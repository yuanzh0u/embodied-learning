DROID-SLAM in the Wild

ML
Moyang LiZihan ZhuMarc PollefeysDaniel Barath

Abstract

We present a robust, real-time RGB SLAM system that handles dynamic environments by leveraging differentiable Uncertainty-aware Bundle Adjustment. Traditional SLAM methods typically assume static scenes, leading to tracking failures in the presence of motion. Recent dynamic SLAM approaches attempt to address this challenge using predefined dynamic priors or uncertainty-aware mapping, but they remain limited when confronted with unknown dynamic objects or highly cluttered scenes where geometric mapping becomes unreliable. In contrast, our method estimates per-pixel uncertainty by exploiting multi-view visual feature inconsistency, enabling robust tracking and reconstruction even in real-world environments. The proposed system achieves state-of-the-art camera poses and scene geometry in cluttered dynamic scenarios while running in real time at around 10 FPS. Code and datasets are available at this https URL.

AI Overview

Concurrent mapping and localization within a single system, known as SLAM, is a cornerstone of modern robotics and autonomous systems. While SLAM systems have matured significantly for static environments, the "real world" is often crowded with moving pedestrians, vehicles, and changing conditions. Traditional methods frequently fail when their assumption of a static scene is violated by such dynamic elements. To address this, researchers from ETH Zurich and Microsoft introduced DROID-W, a visual SLAM system designed to operate robustly "in the wild."

Figure 1: DROID-W handles unconstrained dynamic environments by estimating camera poses, 3D point clouds, and per-pixel uncertainty maps simultaneously, effectively ignoring moving distractors.

The Challenge of Dynamic SLAM

The core problem in visual SLAM is determining where a camera is (pose estimation) and what the world looks like (mapping) based on the movement of pixels across frames. Standard algorithms assume that if a pixel moves, it is because the camera moved relative to a fixed background. However, if a car drives past a stationary robot, the system might misinterpret the car's motion as the robot's own movement, leading to "tracking drift" or a complete failure to localize.

Existing solutions often try to detect moving objects (like cars or people) using pre-trained AI models to mask them out. This works if the model knows what to look for, but fails in "unconstrained" environments with unusual moving objects or in cluttered scenes where masks are imprecise. Other methods try to model "uncertainty"—assigning a lower weight to pixels that don't seem to fit the global motion model. However, these uncertainty measures are often tied to the quality of the 3D map itself; if the map is poor, the uncertainty becomes unreliable. DROID-W addresses these issues by decoupling uncertainty from the map and using high-level visual features to detect inconsistencies.

A critical question is how to determine which pixels are "uncertain." Rather than looking at simple pixel differences, DROID-W uses DINOv2 features. These are high-level mathematical descriptions of image patches that capture semantic information rather than just color.

Implementation and Regularization

To handle the scale ambiguity inherent in monocular (single-camera) SLAM, DROID-W incorporates monocular depth estimates DtD_tDt​ from a pre-trained model called Metric3D. This provides a "hint" about the scene's geometry, which is used as a regularization term:

This prevents the system from drifting in large-scale outdoor scenes or when moving objects dominate the view.

The system is designed for real-time performance. By implementing the uncertainty optimization in CUDA (a language for parallel processing on GPUs), DROID-W can process sequences at roughly 10 to 15 frames per second. This is significantly faster than many recent state-of-the-art methods that rely on complex "3D Gaussian Splatting" or "Neural Radiance Fields" (NeRF), which often take seconds to process a single frame.

Performance in the Wild

The researchers evaluated DROID-W on several datasets, including standard indoor benchmarks like TUM RGB-D and Bonn. However, to truly test "in-the-wild" performance, they captured a new outdoor dataset (DROID-W) featuring long trajectories and high-speed motion, and even tested the system on complex YouTube action scenes.

Figure 3: Qualitative results on a high-motion sequence. The system accurately identifies the moving person as highly uncertain (warm colors) while maintaining low uncertainty for the static room.

Key Results

  • Accuracy: On the Bonn dynamic dataset, DROID-W achieved an average Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) of 2.30 cm, outperforming specialized dynamic SLAM systems like WildGS-SLAM and DynaMoN.
  • Outdoor Robustness: In large-scale outdoor environments (the DROID-W dataset), the system significantly outperformed the baseline DROID-SLAM. While the original system often failed due to moving cars and pedestrians, DROID-W maintained a stable trajectory by correctly identifying and downweighting those dynamic elements.
  • Efficiency: The system is roughly 40 times faster than competing uncertainty-aware SLAM methods (like WildGS-SLAM), making it feasible for actual robotic deployment.

Conclusion

DROID-W demonstrates that by decoupling uncertainty estimation from the mapping process and leveraging high-level visual features, a SLAM system can achieve robustness in environments it was never explicitly trained for. By moving away from rigid "static world" assumptions and predefined object masks, it provides a more general-purpose solution for robots and vehicles navigating the complex, unpredictable reality of the world. Its ability to handle "unconstrained" motion—from a person running in a hallway to cars on a busy street—marks a step toward truly reliable autonomous navigation "in the wild."
