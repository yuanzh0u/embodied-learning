Abstract

Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) in continuous environments requires an agent to ground instructions in egocentric observations while maintaining spatial understanding across long action sequences. Recent navigation foundation models have shown strong progress by scaling vision-language models, but they often learn navigation primarily as direct action generation, without explicitly modeling world states or predicting their future evolution. We introduce FutureNav, a VLM-based unified world-action modeling framework for vision-and-language navigation. Specifically, FutureNav jointly encodes text, visual, and spatial features and feeds them into the LLM, and optimizes four objectives for simultaneous world and action modeling: an action policy objective for navigation action prediction, inverse and forward dynamics objectives for modeling state transitions, and a future generation objective for predicting future spatial states. This unified architecture strengthens action prediction while explicitly modeling the world, without sacrificing inference speed. Extensive experiments show that, with only a 4B-scale backbone, FutureNav achieves state-of-the-art performance on multiple VLN benchmarks and substantially outperforms prior VLN methods, paving the way toward future world-action models for VLN. We will release the code and models to support future research.

AI Overview

Understanding Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments

Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) is a fundamental task in embodied AI, requiring an agent to navigate through 3D environments based on natural language instructions. In continuous environments (VLN-CE), this task is particularly challenging because the agent must handle a vast space of possible movements and visual changes, unlike discrete environments where navigation is simplified to jumping between predefined nodes.

Recent advancements have seen the rise of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) adapted for navigation. These models typically learn a direct mapping from visual observations to actions. While effective at scale, this "observation-to-action" approach often ignores how the agent's actions actually change the world state. On the other hand, "world models" focus on predicting these changes—imagining what the environment will look like after a certain action—but they are often implemented as separate, complex modules that add significant computational burden during navigation.

FutureNav addresses this gap by introducing a unified world-action modeling framework. It integrates the strengths of VLMs with explicit world modeling objectives. Instead of just predicting the next move, FutureNav is trained to understand the relationship between its actions and the resulting environmental transitions. Importantly, it achieves this without requiring any extra computation during the actual navigation process, as the world-modeling components are only active during the training phase.

The Architecture of FutureNav

The FutureNav framework is built upon a VLM backbone, which processes both text instructions and visual frame histories. To enhance the agent's spatial awareness, the architecture incorporates a frozen spatial encoder, specifically a Video-Geometry-Grounded Transformer (VGGT). This choice is motivated by the need for features that capture the geometric structure of the scene, which standard visual encoders might overlook.

The input process involves several steps. First, the natural language instruction xxx and the history of egocentric observations Ot={I1,…,It}O_t = \{I_1, \dots, I_t\}Ot​={I1​,…,It​} at time ttt are tokenized. The visual encoder of the VLM produces visual token embeddings EqtE_{q_t}Eqt​​. Simultaneously, the frozen spatial encoder processes the same frames to produce spatial tokens GtG_tGt​.

To combine these different types of information, a lightweight MLP, referred to as Mergerθ\text{Merger}_{\theta}Mergerθ​, projects the spatial tokens into the language model's hidden space, resulting in EgtE_{g_t}Egt​​. These spatial features are then fused with the VLM's original visual tokens using a residual addition:

where α\alphaα is a scalar weight (set to 0.2). This method ensures that the pretrained capabilities of the VLM are preserved while being augmented with explicit geometric information. This combined representation is then fed into the core language model of the VLM to predict the next action ata_tat​.

Unified World-Action Modeling Objectives

The defining feature of FutureNav is its use of four distinct training objectives that work together to create a robust understanding of the agent-environment interaction. These objectives are implemented as heads that operate on the internal hidden states of the language model.

  1. Action Policy (LpolicyL_{\text{policy}}Lpolicy​): This is the standard navigation objective. It supervises the model to predict the correct next action (e.g., MOVE_FORWARD, TURN_LEFT) given the current instruction and observation history.
  2. Forward Dynamics (LforwardL_{\text{forward}}Lforward​): This objective models how an action changes the world. It takes the hidden state of the current observation hobsth_{\text{obs}_t}hobst​​ and the hidden state of the action hactth_{\text{act}_t}hactt​​ to predict the spatial feature of the next state z^t+1\hat{z}_{t+1}z^t+1​. By comparing this prediction to the actual future state zt+1z_{t+1}zt+1​, the model learns the visual consequences of its decisions.
  3. Inverse Dynamics (LinverseL_{\text{inverse}}Linverse​): This head works in the opposite direction. It looks at the transition between two consecutive observations (hˉvt−1\bar{h}_{v_{t-1}}hˉvt−1​​ and hˉvt\bar{h}_{v_t}hˉvt​​) and tries to infer what action caused that change. This reinforces the model's understanding of how specific visual changes relate to specific actions.
  4. Future Generation (LgenL_{\text{gen}}Lgen​): This head predicts the next spatial state z^gent+1\hat{z}_{\text{gen}_{t+1}}z^gent+1​​ based on the current context hctxth_{\text{ctx}_t}hctxt​​ and observation hˉvt\bar{h}_{v_t}hˉvt​​, but without being told what the action is. This helps the model learn a prior for how the environment naturally evolves and maintains scene continuity.

Efficient Learning and Training

The final training loss is a weighted sum of these four objectives:

L=Lpolicy+λfLforward+λiLinverse+λgLgenL = L_{\text{policy}} + \lambda_f L_{\text{forward}} + \lambda_i L_{\text{inverse}} + \lambda_g L_{\text{gen}}L=Lpolicy​+λf​Lforward​+λi​Linverse​+λg​Lgen​

where λf\lambda_fλf​, λi\lambda_iλi​, and λg\lambda_gλg​ are hyperparameters that balance the auxiliary tasks. This unified loss function ensures that as the model learns to navigate, it simultaneously builds a latent representation that is consistent with the laws of its environment.

One of the most significant advantages of this setup is inference efficiency. Unlike traditional world models that must generate or imagine future states during navigation to plan a path, FutureNav only uses these auxiliary heads during the training phase. When the agent is actually navigating an environment, these heads are discarded, and only the action policy branch is used. This allows FutureNav to benefit from the depth of world modeling without the associated latency or memory overhead at runtime.

Empirical data shows that this multi-task approach also speeds up the learning process. FutureNav achieves higher success rates much earlier in the training schedule compared to models trained only on the action policy.

Performance and Empirical Evaluation

FutureNav was evaluated on two major continuous VLN benchmarks: R2R-CE (Room-to-Room) and RxR-CE (Room-Across-Room). These datasets involve complex instructions in diverse indoor settings.

The results demonstrate that FutureNav sets a new standard for performance in this domain. Even with a smaller 4-billion parameter backbone (FutureNav-4B), it outperformed 7-billion parameter models like JanusVLN. In the "0K setting"—where the model is trained only on the specific dataset without massive external navigation data—FutureNav-4B showed a 4.4% improvement in Success Rate (SR) over larger competitors on R2R-CE.

Ablation studies confirmed that every component of the world-action modeling contributes to the final performance. Adding forward dynamics, for instance, provided the single largest boost, but the best results were only achieved when all four objectives were used in unison. This suggests that action prediction, state transition modeling, and scene continuity are complementary skills for a navigating agent.

Qualitatively, agents powered by FutureNav exhibit much smoother and more accurate paths. They are better at recognizing landmarks mentioned in instructions and are significantly more precise at deciding exactly when to stop at a destination, a common failure point for many VLN systems.

Real-World Generalization and Zero-Shot Performance

A critical test for any embodied AI model is its ability to transition from simulation to the real world (sim-to-real). FutureNav was tested in various real-world environments, including offices, labs, and outdoor plazas, using a quadruped robot.

Remarkably, the model was able to follow complex instructions in these unseen environments without any real-world fine-tuning. This zero-shot generalization indicates that the world-action representations learned in simulation are robust enough to handle the variations in lighting, texture, and physical layouts found in reality.

The agent successfully navigated around obstacles, recognized real-world landmarks like vending machines and coffee shops, and correctly interpreted spatial directions. This capability stems from the model's unified understanding of how its movements relate to visual changes, a skill that is inherently transferable across environments.

Conclusion: Future Implications for Embodied AI

FutureNav represents a shift in how we approach the training of navigation agents. By proving that explicit world modeling can be unified with action prediction in a standard VLM framework, the researchers have shown a path toward more intelligent and efficient embodied models.

The key takeaways from this work are:

  • Unified Modeling: Learning "what will happen" is just as important as learning "what to do," and these can be trained together.
  • Efficiency: World modeling doesn't have to be slow; training-time auxiliary tasks can enrich a model's representation without slowing down its real-time performance.
  • Spatial Awareness: Incorporating specialized spatial encoders like VGGT provides the geometric foundation necessary for complex navigation that standard visual encoders might miss.
  • Scalability: Better modeling techniques can allow smaller, more efficient models to outperform larger ones that lack a structured understanding of the world.

As embodied AI continues to move toward more complex and dynamic settings, frameworks like FutureNav that emphasize a fundamental understanding of world dynamics will likely become the foundation for the next generation of autonomous agents.
