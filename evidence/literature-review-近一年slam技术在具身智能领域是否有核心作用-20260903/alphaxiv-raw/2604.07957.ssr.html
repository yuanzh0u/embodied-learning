WorldMAP: Bootstrapping Vision-Language Navigation Trajectory Prediction with Generative World Models

Abstract

Vision-language models (VLMs) and generative world models are opening new opportunities for embodied navigation. VLMs are increasingly used as direct planners or trajectory predictors, while world models support look-ahead reasoning by imagining future views. Yet predicting a reliable trajectory from a single egocentric observation remains challenging. Current VLMs often generate unstable trajectories, and world models, though able to synthesize plausible futures, do not directly provide the grounded signals needed for navigation learning. This raises a central question: how can generated futures be turned into supervision for grounded trajectory prediction? We present WorldMAP, a teacher--student framework that converts world-model-generated futures into persistent semantic-spatial structure and planning-derived supervision. Its world-model-driven teacher builds semantic-spatial memory from generated videos, grounds task-relevant targets and obstacles, and produces trajectory pseudo-labels through explicit planning. A lightweight student with a multi-hypothesis trajectory head is then trained to predict navigation trajectories directly from vision-language inputs. On Target-Bench, WorldMAP achieves the best ADE and FDE among compared methods, reducing ADE by 18.0% and FDE by 42.1% relative to the best competing baseline, while lifting a small open-source VLM to DTW performance competitive with proprietary models. More broadly, the results suggest that, in embodied navigation, the value of world models may lie less in supplying action-ready imagined evidence than in synthesizing structured supervision for navigation learning.

AI Overview

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) represents a fundamental challenge in embodied artificial intelligence: the ability of an autonomous agent to navigate through complex, previously unseen environments guided by natural-language instructions. Achieving this requires the agent to understand semantic concepts, such as identifying a "vending machine" or "the leftmost parking space," while simultaneously respecting the physical geometry of the world, such as floor traversability and obstacle avoidance.

Figure 1: The WorldMAP framework leverages a teacher-student distillation process where a generative world model synthesizes structured navigation supervision.

Traditionally, navigation systems have been split into classical pipelines—which rely on explicit geometric maps and analytical planners—and modern end-to-end learning approaches. While classical methods are reliable, they often require extensive pre-mapping of an environment. Conversely, recent Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate impressive semantic reasoning but often fail to generate stable, grounded trajectories. They might identify a target correctly but suggest a path that clips through a wall or stops far from the objective. WorldMAP introduces a framework that bridges this gap by using generative world models not just to "imagine" the future, but to create high-quality, structured supervision for training robust navigation policies.

The Limitations of Current Paradigms

Existing approaches to language-grounded navigation typically fall into two categories, both of which face distinct hurdles. The first category involves using VLMs as direct trajectory predictors. While these models possess a vast knowledge base, they lack the spatial grounding necessary for precise movement. In benchmarks like Target-Bench, direct VLM predictions often exhibit high errors in goal localization and fail to maintain traversability.

The second category utilizes generative world models to simulate potential futures. These models can synthesize visually plausible video sequences of what might happen if an agent moves in a certain direction. However, simply showing an agent these "imagined" futures is often insufficient. Research has shown that ungated imagination can introduce misleading information, and the raw visual output of a world model does not inherently provide the structured planning signals—like clear obstacle boundaries or optimal paths—required for a navigation agent to learn effectively.

WorldMAP addresses these issues by repositioning the role of world models. Instead of using them as transient evidence at test time, WorldMAP uses them as "supervision engines." This approach transforms the visual imagination of a world model into persistent, semantic-spatial structures that can be used to teach a lightweight student model how to navigate.

The WorldMAP Framework

The architecture of WorldMAP is inspired by the "World-Memory-Action-Perception" decomposition for autonomous intelligence. It operates as a teacher-student distillation framework. The "Teacher" is a sophisticated, computationally heavy pipeline that uses world models and geometric planners to generate high-quality navigation labels. The "Student" is a lightweight Vision-Language Model that learns from these labels to perform efficient navigation at inference time.

Figure 2: The WorldMAP pipeline consists of a world-model-driven teacher that generates pseudo-labels and a student VLM that learns to predict trajectories.

This structure follows a "fast-slow" system design. The teacher represents the "slow" system, performing complex reasoning, 3D reconstruction, and multi-view grounding to determine the best path. The student represents the "fast" system, which amortizes the teacher’s expensive computation into a single forward pass, allowing for real-time navigation without the need for world-model generation during deployment.

The World-Model-Driven Teacher

The teacher's primary task is to convert raw future video generations into "structured trajectory supervision." This involves three critical stages: World Construction, Task-Aware Grounding, and Geometric Planning.

World Construction and Memory

The process begins with a generative world model (such as Sora or Wan2.1) creating short future videos from the agent's current viewpoint. These videos represent different potential movements and viewpoints. To make sense of these frames, the teacher uses Depth Anything 3 to estimate monocular depth for every frame, backprojecting them into a shared 3D representation. Simultaneously, each frame is stored in a semantic-spatial memory, which includes visual embeddings, camera poses, and automatically generated captions.

Task-Aware Grounding

To navigate toward a specific target, the teacher must ground the language instruction in the synthesized world. It uses the semantic-spatial memory to retrieve the frames most relevant to the instruction. An open-vocabulary segmentation model, UniPixel, then identifies the target regions and potential obstacles across these multi-view frames. By projecting these 2D masks into the shared 3D space and eventually onto a 2D Bird's-Eye View (BEV) grid, the teacher creates a metric-preserving map of the environment.

Figure 3: The teacher synthesizes a shared Bird's-Eye View (BEV) by aggregating semantic grounding and geometric evidence from generated future views.

Geometric Planning

Once the BEV cost map is constructed—with designated target zones and blocked obstacle regions—the teacher applies the Fast Marching Method (FMM). FMM is a computational technique used to find the shortest path from the agent's current position to the target while avoiding obstacles. This planned path is smoothed and converted into a sequence of waypoints, forming the "teacher trajectory." This trajectory acts as a high-quality pseudo-label that tells the student exactly where it should have walked.

Distilling Knowledge into the Student

The student model is a compact VLM (based on the Qwen3-VL-8B backbone) equipped with a specialized trajectory decoder. During training, the student is presented with the original first-person image and the language instruction. It is then trained to predict the same waypoint sequence that the teacher generated through its complex planning process.

Multi-Hypothesis Prediction

Because navigation instructions can sometimes be ambiguous, the student uses a multi-hypothesis head. It predicts KKK potential waypoint sequences, each with an associated confidence score. During training, the model uses a selection-based loss function. Quality Curation

Not all teacher-generated trajectories are perfect. World-model generations can sometimes be inconsistent or fail to capture the scene geometry correctly. To ensure the student learns only from high-quality data, a VLM-based quality assessment protocol is used. Each teacher trajectory is judged on five criteria: task success, stopping accuracy, collision safety, trajectory naturalness, and cross-view consistency. Only trajectories labeled as "usable" are used for the primary training of the student.

Experimental Results

The WorldMAP framework was evaluated on Target-Bench, a benchmark focusing on navigation in unstructured indoor and outdoor settings. Performance was measured using Average Displacement Error (ADE\text{ADE}ADE), Final Displacement Error (FDE\text{FDE}FDE), and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW\text{DTW}DTW).

The results demonstrated that WorldMAP significantly outperforms direct VLM prediction. For instance, the WorldMAP student achieved an ADE\text{ADE}ADE of 42.06, which is an 18% improvement over the best proprietary model, Gemini-3-Pro (which had an ADE\text{ADE}ADE of 51.27). In terms of FDE\text{FDE}FDE, the improvement was even more pronounced, with WorldMAP reducing the error by 42.1% compared to Gemini-3-Pro.

Figure 5: Qualitative results show that WorldMAP (green) generates smoother, safer, and more accurate paths than baseline VLMs and other world-model methods.

A key finding was the comparison with other world-model-augmented methods like MindJourney. MindJourney, which provides imagined views at test time, actually performed worse than direct proprietary models. This supports the WorldMAP hypothesis: world models are most effective when used to generate structured training supervision (the teacher approach) rather than being used as raw evidence for real-time planning.

The Significance of Structured Supervision

WorldMAP represents a shift in how we think about "imagination" in AI. In many previous works, imagination was treated as a way to "see" into the future during an action. In WorldMAP, imagination is a tool for "learning" from the future. By converting transient video frames into persistent 3D and BEV representations, the framework provides the student with the benefits of a SLAM-like geometric understanding without requiring the student to perform SLAM itself.

Furthermore, this framework allows smaller, open-source models to compete with the industry's largest proprietary models. By giving an 8B-parameter model high-quality, planning-derived supervision, it can learn to respect physical constraints that even 100B+ parameter models struggle with when predicting trajectories directly.

Ultimately, WorldMAP suggests that the path toward more capable embodied agents lies in combining the vast semantic knowledge of VLMs with the structured, predictive power of generative world models through the lens of teacher-student distillation. This approach provides a scalable way to bridge the gap between "understanding" a scene and "acting" within it safely and precisely.
