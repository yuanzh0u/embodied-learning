GaussLite: Online Task-Conditioned 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Robotic Mapping

AT
Annika Thomas
MP
Mason PetersonJonathan P. How

Abstract

Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) systems distribute representation capacity uniformly across a scene, ignoring the fact that many downstream robotic tasks engage only a fraction of the reconstructed geometry. This causes valuable onboard compute to be allocated towards optimizing irrelevant parts of the scene, either limiting online capacity or under-optimizing the most relevant parts of the scene. We introduce GaussLite, a task-driven 3DGS mapping system that conditions its representation density on a natural-language task specification. Given a posed RGB-D stream and a task such as "prepare to pick up the object on the desk," GaussLite uses a one-shot LLM parser to extract target and anchor objects, which are grounded per-frame by an open-vocabulary detector and segmented to produce per-pixel relevance masks in real time. The mapper allocates seeding density, gradient flow and scaling by task relevance. At matched Gaussian budget and real-time mapping at 4 Hz on resource-constrained hardware, GaussLite outperforms baselines on ROI PSNR on the Replica Dataset by an average +2.72 dB and on a real-hardware demonstration in indoor and outdoor settings by +2.23 dB. We further show that two task-specialized agents' maps can be fused into a single shared map via per-voxel voting on active-optimization counts in real time, outperforming concatenation by +3.42 dB while only sharing an average 7.08% of the map.

AI Overview

Task-Conditioned Representation in Robotic Mapping

Robotic systems operating in complex, dynamic environments require efficient ways to represent their surroundings. Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a powerful technique for creating high-fidelity, photorealistic 3D reconstructions. Unlike traditional voxel-based or mesh-based methods, 3DGS represents the world as a collection of thousands or millions of overlapping 3D Gaussians. While this approach has enabled real-time rendering and high-quality mapping, most current systems treat every part of a scene with equal importance.

Figure 1: GaussLite enables a robot to focus its mapping efforts on task-relevant regions, such as specific objects mentioned in a natural language command, rather than uniformly reconstructing the entire scene.

This uniform allocation of computational resources is often inefficient for robotics. A robot tasked with picking up a specific cup does not need the same level of detail for the distant ceiling or the floor in another room as it does for the area immediately surrounding the cup. GaussLite addresses this by introducing a task-conditioned approach to 3DGS mapping. By directing the system's "attention" toward geometry relevant to a specific goal, the mapping process becomes more efficient, achieving higher fidelity where it matters most while remaining within the constraints of real-time onboard processing.

The Principle of Task-Driven Attention

The core philosophy of GaussLite is inspired by biological vision. Human eyes do not see the entire field of view with equal clarity; instead, the fovea provides high-acuity vision in a small, central region, while the periphery remains coarse. This biological "foveation" is dynamic—our eyes move to focus on what is relevant to our current task.

GaussLite translates this principle into the domain of robotic mapping. It utilizes a natural language interface to define the robot's "focus." When a user provides a task like "prepare to pick up the object on the desk," the system identifies the relevant objects and spatial relationships. This high-level understanding is then used to generate a per-pixel relevance map w(u,v)w(u,v)w(u,v), which serves as an attention signal for the underlying 3DGS mapper.

Current 3DGS-SLAM systems like SplaTAM or Gaussian-SLAM generally lack this task-awareness. They distribute their "seed" Gaussians and optimization steps uniformly across the observed frames. By contrast, GaussLite splits its resources: it maintains a coarse background "scaffold" while concentrating dense, high-resolution Gaussians in the regions identified as task-relevant.

From Natural Language to Spatial Attention

To bridge the gap between human instructions and pixel-level mapping, GaussLite employs a structured front-end pipeline. This process happens in several distinct steps:

  1. Instruction Parsing: A lightweight Large Language Model (LLM), specifically Phi-3-mini, parses a free-form instruction into a structured graph. This graph, denoted as Gtask={Otarget,Oanchor,R}G_{task} = \{O_{target}, O_{anchor}, R\}Gtask​={Otarget​,Oanchor​,R}, identifies the target objects, anchor objects used for spatial reference, and the relationships RRR (e.g., "ON", "NEAR") between them.
  2. Visual Grounding: The system uses Grounding DINO to detect 2D bounding boxes for the targets and anchors in the camera feed. These boxes are then refined into precise pixel-level masks using FastSAM.
  3. Spatial Reasoning in 3D: To ensure the detections match the user's intent, the system evaluates the spatial predicates in 3D. It uses the current depth map to project the 2D masks into 3D world coordinates. If a detected "cup" is not actually "ON" the detected "desk" in 3D space, it is filtered out.
  4. Relevance Mapping: The final, filtered masks are combined into the binary relevance map w(u,v)w(u,v)w(u,v). Pixels within the identified task region are marked as relevant (w(u,v)=1w(u,v) = 1w(u,v)=1), while others are marked as background (w(u,v)=0w(u,v) = 0w(u,v)=0).

Figure 2: The pipeline for generating a binary relevance map from natural language instructions. It involves LLM parsing, 2D object detection/segmentation, and 3D spatial filtering.

Relevance-Driven Gaussian Seeding and Optimization

Once the relevance map is established, it governs how the 3DGS map is expanded and refined. Standard 3DGS defines each Gaussian by its position, rotation, scale, opacity, and color. GaussLite adds two critical non-differentiable attributes to each Gaussian:

  • An active flag ai∈{0,1}a_i \in \{0, 1\}ai​∈{0,1}: Set when the Gaussian is first created (seeded), based on the value of w(u,v)w(u,v)w(u,v) at that pixel.
  • An active-optimization counter κi∈Z≥0\kappa_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}κi​∈Z≥0​: This tracks how many times an active Gaussian has been updated during optimization.

Strategic Seeding

The system manages its Gaussian budget NNN using ROI Seed Budgeting. A ratio ρ\rhoρ determines how many new Gaussians are allocated to the relevant regions (NROI=ρNN_{ROI} = \rho NNROI​=ρN) versus the background (Nbg=(1−ρ)NN_{bg} = (1 - \rho) NNbg​=(1−ρ)N).

Beyond simple budgeting, GaussLite uses Stratified Initial Scaling (SIS). Background Gaussians are initialized with larger scales to cover more area with fewer primitives, while ROI Gaussians are initialized smaller to allow for finer detail. Furthermore, Gradient-Focused Densification (GFD) biases the placement of seeds toward areas with high image gradients (edges and textures) using a probability distribution derived from a Sobel filter E(u,v)+ϵE(u,v) + \epsilonE(u,v)+ϵ.

Selective Optimization

During the optimization phase, the system first performs a brief "warmup" on the entire frame to ensure global consistency. However, for the majority of the optimization iterations, only Gaussians with ai=1a_i = 1ai​=1 are updated. By restricting gradients to the active Gaussians, the system focuses its entire computational "energy" on the task-relevant geometry. This results in sharper textures and more accurate geometry for the objects the robot needs to interact with.

Collaborative Mapping in Multi-Agent Teams

In scenarios involving multiple robots, GaussLite enables efficient map sharing. Instead of exchanging massive, uniform 3D models, robots can specialize based on their individual tasks. For instance, one robot might focus on a "trash bin" while another focuses on a "chair."

The fusion process uses the active-optimization counter κi\kappa_iκi​ as a proxy for quality. When two robots provide data for the same physical space, the system performs a "Voxel Vote." The world is divided into a grid, and for each voxel, the system selects the Gaussians from the agent that has the highest accumulated κi\kappa_iκi​ for that region. This ensures that the most "well-trained" representation is preserved.

Figure 3: Multi-agent fusion utilizes task-specific counters to merge specialized maps, allowing robots to share only the most relevant, high-fidelity parts of their reconstructions.

This method is highly communication-efficient. Since robots only need to share the subsets of their maps that are task-relevant and well-optimized, the amount of data exchanged is significantly reduced (by over 90% in reported experiments) compared to sharing full reconstructions.

Performance and Evaluation

The system was evaluated on both synthetic (Replica) and real-world (Campus) datasets, comparing it against established 3DGS-SLAM baselines such as SplaTAM, Gaussian-SLAM, and MonoGS.

Quantitative Results

In task-relevant regions (ROI), GaussLite consistently outperformed the baselines. On the Replica dataset, it achieved a mean ROI Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) of 29.81 dB, which is approximately 1.25 dB to 3.71 dB higher than the comparison methods. In real-world campus scenes, the improvements were even more pronounced, with GaussLite often showing over 2 dB improvement in ROI PSNR.

Qualitative Fidelity

Visually, the difference is clear in the rendering of small details. While baseline methods often produce blurry or "melted" reconstructions of task-relevant objects due to limited local capacity, GaussLite maintains sharp edges and clear textures.

Figure 4: A comparison of rendering quality. GaussLite preserves fine details in the task-relevant regions (inset boxes) compared to uniform mapping baselines.

Ablation Study

A component-wise analysis confirmed that each part of the pipeline—seed budgeting, stratified scaling, and selective optimization—contributes to the final performance. The study highlighted a clear trade-off: as the system focuses more on the ROI, the ROI PSNR increases significantly, while the full-image PSNR (which includes irrelevant background) stays stable or slightly decreases, reflecting the intentional reallocation of resources.

Figure 5: Ablation study showing the cumulative benefit of adding task-conditioned components. Note how ROI PSNR increases as more specialized features are enabled.

Summary of Contributions

GaussLite represents a shift in how 3DGS can be applied to robotics. Its primary contributions include:

  • Task-Conditioned Mapping: The first 3DGS system to accept natural language instructions to dynamically allocate representation capacity online.
  • Efficiency on Constraints: By focusing optimization on active Gaussians, it achieves higher fidelity in important areas while staying within the compute and memory budgets of robotic platforms.
  • Specialized Seeding and Scaling: The introduction of ROI-specific seeding budgets and stratified scaling to handle varying levels of detail across a scene.
  • Weighted Multi-Agent Fusion: A novel κi\kappa_iκi​-based voxel voting mechanism that allows multi-robot teams to efficiently merge task-specialized maps with minimal communication overhead.

By treating the 3D representation as a "cognitive map" that adapts to the task at hand, GaussLite provides a more practical and effective way for robots to perceive and interact with their environments.
