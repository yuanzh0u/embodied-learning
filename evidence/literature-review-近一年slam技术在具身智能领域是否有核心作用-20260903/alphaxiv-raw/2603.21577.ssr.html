Abstract

Despite the widespread adoption of MLLMs in embodied agents, their capabilities remain largely confined to reactive planning from immediate observations, consistently failing in spatial reasoning across extensive spatiotemporal scales. Cognitive science reveals that Biological Intelligence (BI) thrives on "mental navigation": the strategic construction of spatial representations from experience and the subsequent mental simulation of paths prior to action. To bridge the gap between AI and BI, we introduce Video2Mental, a pioneering benchmark for evaluating the mental navigation capabilities of MLLMs. The task requires constructing hierarchical cognitive maps from long egocentric videos and generating landmark-based path plans step by step, with planning accuracy verified through simulator-based physical interaction. Our benchmarking results reveal that mental navigation capability does not naturally emerge from standard pre-training. Frontier MLLMs struggle profoundly with zero-shot structured spatial representation, and their planning accuracy decays precipitously over extended horizons. To overcome this, we propose NavMind, a reasoning model that internalizes mental navigation using explicit, fine-grained cognitive maps as learnable intermediate representations. Through a difficulty-stratified progressive supervised fine-tuning paradigm, NavMind effectively bridges the gap between raw perception and structured planning. Experiments demonstrate that NavMind achieves superior mental navigation capabilities, significantly outperforming frontier commercial and spatial MLLMs.

AI Overview

The Challenge of Long-Horizon Spatial Reasoning

Current developments in embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) have leveraged Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to improve how agents understand their surroundings and plan actions. These models allow robots to translate visual observations into language-based instructions or task plans. However, most existing MLLM deployments are limited to "reactive" planning, where the agent makes decisions based on immediate, local observations. This approach often fails when tasks require reasoning over long distances or extended periods of time.

In contrast, biological intelligence exhibits a capability known as "mental navigation." Humans and animals do not just react to what is right in front of them; they build "cognitive maps"—internal, structured representations of their environment based on past experiences. These maps allow them to mentally simulate paths and plan routes to distant goals before they even take a step. This research identifies a significant gap: while MLLMs are proficient at short-range tasks, they lack the ability to internalize spatial representations from streaming video to perform global, proactive planning.

This work addresses this limitation by formally defining the "mental navigation" task and developing a benchmark to evaluate it. The core idea is to move beyond simple question-answering about a scene and instead require models to construct a topological map and generate a physically executable navigation plan. By shifting the focus from local reaction to global mental simulation, the authors aim to bring AI closer to the spatial reasoning capabilities observed in nature.

Defining the Mental Navigation Task

The research defines mental navigation as a two-stage cognitive process where an MLLM must first build a map and then use it to plan. The task is formulated with an input egocentric video sequence V={f1,...,fT}V = \{f_1, ..., f_T\}V={f1​,...,fT​} and a natural language query q=(ssrc,stgt)q = (s_{src}, s_{tgt})q=(ssrc​,stgt​), which specifies the starting location and the target destination.

The model is required to produce a structured output that includes two components:

  1. Hierarchical Cognitive Map (MMM): This is a structured textual representation that organizes the environment into Regions, Landmarks, and Objects.
  2. Navigation Reasoning Chain (WWW): This is a sequence of landmark-grounded steps that describe the path from the source to the target.

Each step in the reasoning chain Pi=(lmi,semi,reli,bboxi)P_i = (lm_i, sem_i, rel_i, bbox_i)Pi​=(lmi​,semi​,reli​,bboxi​) specifies a landmark, its semantic category, its spatial relationship to the agent (e.g., "to the front-left"), and its estimated spatial bounding box. This structured approach forces the model to externalize its "mental" process, making its spatial understanding transparent and verifiable. By requiring the map to be generated before the plan, the task ensures the model is not merely guessing but is operating on a stored spatial representation.

Video2Mental: A Benchmark for Long-Horizon Planning

To evaluate this new task, the authors created Video2Mental, a large-scale benchmark dataset derived from high-fidelity 3D indoor simulators (Habitat-Sim using HM3D and MP3D datasets). The benchmark is designed to be significantly more challenging than existing spatial reasoning datasets. It contains 23,700 samples, each featuring an egocentric video that can last over five minutes.

The benchmark construction involves several key steps to ensure spatial accuracy:

  • Hierarchical Mapping: The environment is automatically partitioned into functional regions (like bedrooms or kitchens) using clustering algorithms. Within these regions, objects are ranked by their visual and semantic importance to be designated as "landmarks."
  • Coordinate Grounding: The data uses a strict right-handed coordinate system to represent 3D space accurately. This allows for precise calculation of distances and bearings between objects.
  • Spatiotemporal Stratification: The tasks are categorized by path length—Short (up to 6m), Middle (up to 10m), and Long (up to 48m). This stratification allows researchers to observe how a model's performance degrades as the complexity of the spatial reasoning increases.

The benchmark evaluates models not just on the text they generate, but on the physical validity of that text. By deploying the generated plans in a simulator, the researchers can determine if the model's "mental" path is actually navigable in the real (simulated) world.

Measuring Mental Navigation Success

Evaluating mental navigation requires a multi-dimensional approach that combines language accuracy with physical execution. The research uses two main tracks for evaluation:

Static Textual Evaluation

This track assesses the accuracy of the generated map and plan against a ground-truth reference. Key metrics include:

  • Landmark-F1: Measures how well the model identified the correct set of landmarks.
  • Navigation Error (NENENE): The Euclidean distance between where the model thinks the target is and where it actually is.
  • Target Success Rate (SRtSR_tSRt​): A binary measure of whether the model's predicted target location is within one meter of the true target (NE<1mNE < 1mNE<1m).

Interactive Simulator Evaluation

This track is more rigorous as it tests whether the plan can be executed by a physical agent.

  • Execution-verified Path Success Rate (SRpSR_pSRp​): This metric tracks whether an agent, following the model's plan, actually reaches the destination. A plan might look good in text but fail if it directs the agent through a wall or misses a critical turn.
  • Success weighted by Path Length (SPL): This measures efficiency. It rewards plans that find the shortest path and penalizes those that take long, unnecessary detours.

NavMind: Internalizing Spatial Representations

The authors propose NavMind, a reasoning model designed specifically for the mental navigation task. NavMind is built on the Qwen3-VL architecture and is trained to treat spatial cognition as a decoupled, two-stage process. Mathematically, the model's operation is represented as (M^,W^)=fθ(V,q)(\hat{M}, \hat{W}) = f_{\theta}(V, q)(M^,W^)=fθ​(V,q), where the model fθf_{\theta}fθ​ takes the video and query to produce the map M^\hat{M}M^ and the reasoning chain W^\hat{W}W^.

A key feature of NavMind is its training paradigm, called Cognition-Guided Progressive Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). This process happens in two stages:

  1. Foundational SFT: The model is initially trained on the entire Video2Mental dataset to learn the basic relationship between video frames and spatial descriptions.
  2. Cognition-guided Rejection Sampling (CogRS): The authors observed that many navigation tasks are too simple and don't challenge the model's reasoning. CogRS identifies "difficult" samples by measuring the model's uncertainty (perplexity) on critical decision-making tokens, such as specific landmark names or spatial relations. The model then undergoes a second round of intensive training on these informative, high-difficulty samples.

The loss function for this training balances the accuracy of the map and the reasoning chain:

This ensures the model learns that a good plan is dependent on a high-quality internal map.

Analysis of Model Limitations and Results

The evaluation of existing MLLMs (such as GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro) on the Video2Mental benchmark revealed several important findings:

  • The Emergence Gap: Standard MLLMs performed poorly on the mental navigation task, with success rates often below 6%. This suggests that the ability to mentally navigate and build long-term spatial maps does not simply "emerge" from general vision-language training.
  • The Reasoning Bottleneck: To test whether the models were failing because of vision or reasoning, the researchers provided the models with perfect ground-truth maps (MN w/ GT-Map). Even with perfect "vision" and mapping, most models still struggled to generate executable plans, indicating that the primary deficit lies in the models' internal spatial reasoning logic.
  • Horizon Collapse: As the navigation distance increased, the performance of baseline models dropped significantly. They tended to rely on local heuristics (guessing based on what is nearby) rather than maintaining a consistent global strategy.

In contrast, NavMind demonstrated significantly stronger performance. It achieved a Target Success Rate (SRtSR_tSRt​) over 43% higher than the average baseline and reduced navigation error by several meters. Most importantly, NavMind maintained its performance much better as tasks became longer and more complex, proving that its structured approach to cognitive mapping and specialized training helped it overcome the "horizon collapse" seen in other models.

Significance for Future Embodied AI

This research highlights a path forward for developing more capable autonomous agents. By proving that existing large-scale models lack "mental navigation" capabilities and providing a framework to solve it, the authors offer a blueprint for building "navigation brains."

One practical application demonstrated in the paper is the integration of NavMind with a downstream Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) agent. While standard VLN agents often get lost in complex environments after hundreds of steps, NavMind can act as a high-level guide, providing a landmark-grounded plan that keeps the agent on track. In one example, an agent that failed to find a target after 274 steps was able to reach it in just 34 steps when guided by NavMind's global planning.

The Video2Mental benchmark and the NavMind model represent a shift toward agents that can think ahead and simulate their actions in a mental world model. This approach moves the field of AI closer to the efficiency and proactive planning seen in biological systems, which is essential for robots operating in large, complex, and real-world environments.
