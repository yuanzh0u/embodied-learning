RoSHI: A Versatile Robot-oriented Suit for Human Data In-the-Wild

WM
Wenjing Margaret Mao
JN
Jefferson Ng
LH
Luyang HuDaniel GehrigAntonio Loquercio

Abstract

Scaling up robot learning will likely require human data containing rich and long-horizon interactions in the wild. Existing approaches for collecting such data trade off portability, robustness to occlusion, and global consistency. We introduce RoSHI, a hybrid wearable that fuses low-cost sparse IMUs with the Project Aria glasses to estimate the full 3D pose and body shape of the wearer in a metric global coordinate frame from egocentric perception. This system is motivated by the complementarity of the two sensors: IMUs provide robustness to occlusions and high-speed motions, while egocentric SLAM anchors long-horizon motion and stabilizes upper body pose. We collect a dataset of agile activities to evaluate RoSHI. On this dataset, we generally outperform other egocentric baselines and perform comparably to a state-of-the-art exocentric baseline (SAM3D). Finally, we demonstrate that the motion data recorded from our system are suitable for real-world humanoid policy learning. For videos, data and more, visit the project webpage: this https URL

AI Overview

As the field of robotics moves toward general-purpose humanoid agents, the demand for high-quality human motion data has increased. Teaching a robot to walk, run, or interact with objects often begins with observing how humans perform these tasks. However, capturing this data "in the wild"—outside of laboratory settings and in natural, unconstrained environments—remains a significant technical challenge. Traditional systems are either too expensive, confined to instrumented rooms, or prone to errors during fast and occluded movements.

RoSHI (Robot-oriented Suit for Human Data In-the-Wild) is a hybrid wearable system designed to bridge this gap. Developed by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania, RoSHI combines low-cost inertial measurement units (IMUs) with egocentric (first-person) vision. This combination allows the system to capture metric-scale 3D human body poses and globally consistent trajectories in everyday settings, providing the necessary signals for training humanoid robot policies.

Figure 1: The RoSHI system architecture. It fuses egocentric SLAM from Project Aria glasses with bone orientations from a sparse set of nine low-cost IMUs. The data is processed through an IMU-guided diffusion model to produce globally consistent 3D human motion.

The Challenge of Motion Capture in the Wild

To train a humanoid robot, researchers typically require three types of synchronized information: the 3D human body pose, a globally consistent 6-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) root trajectory (how the person moves through space), and egocentric visual data to provide environmental context. Current technologies often force a compromise between these requirements.

Marker-based systems like Vicon provide high accuracy but require expensive infra-red cameras mounted in a fixed room, making them impossible to use outdoors or in large houses. Conversely, commercial IMU suits (like Xsens) are portable but expensive and suffer from "global drift." Because IMUs only measure relative motion, small errors accumulate over time, making it appear as if the human is drifting through the floor or floating away from their starting position.

Vision-only methods, which use external cameras to track a person, are restricted by the camera's field of view and fail when the person is occluded by furniture or moves too quickly for the camera to track. Egocentric systems, such as head-mounted cameras, provide great environmental context and global positioning through Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), but they struggle to see the wearer's full body, leading to poor estimations of leg or back movements. RoSHI addresses these issues by fusing the complementary strengths of these modalities.

Hardware and System Design

The RoSHI hardware is designed to be accessible and robust. It consists of two primary components:

  1. Project Aria Glasses: These glasses, developed by Meta, serve as the "head" of the system. They provide wide-angle egocentric RGB video and use onboard SLAM to track the user's head position in a global coordinate frame. This provides a stable "anchor" for the rest of the body's motion.
  2. Custom IMU Trackers: The system uses nine wireless IMU trackers placed on the pelvis, thighs, shanks, upper arms, and forearms. Each tracker costs approximately $30\$30$30 USD and is built using a BNO085 sensor, a microcontroller with 2.4 GHz wireless communication, and a battery. The total cost for the IMU subsystem is roughly $350\$350$350 USD, making it significantly more affordable than professional suits that can cost tens of thousands of dollars.

The sensors are synchronized using Unix timestamps. The IMUs provide high-frequency (100 Hz) data on the orientation of individual limbs, which is critical for capturing fast, dynamic motions like jumping or throwing, while the Aria glasses provide the global reference frame.

Reconstructing Motion via Diffusion

At the heart of RoSHI is a motion reconstruction pipeline that translates raw sensor data into a Skinned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model—a standard digital representation of the human body. The system leverages a modified version of "EgoAllo," a diffusion-based generative model.

In a standard diffusion model, the system learns to "denoise" random signals into plausible human poses. RoSHI improves this by adding three specific constraints (guidance) based on the IMU data to ensure the generated pose matches the physical reality of the wearer:

  • Joint Angle Constraint (Ljoint\mathcal{L}_{joint}Ljoint​): The system compares the joint angles (like the elbow or knee bend) predicted by the diffusion model with the actual orientations measured by the IMUs.
  • Relative Orientation Consistency: It ensures that the relative rotation between different body parts (such as the pelvis and the shoulder) matches the IMU measurements.
  • Temporal Consistency (Ltemp\mathcal{L}_{temp}Ltemp​): This ensures that the transition between frames is smooth and physically plausible, preventing the jitter often seen in independent frame-by-frame estimations.

By combining these constraints, RoSHI can reconstruct parts of the body that the head-mounted camera cannot see. For example, if a user is crouching or walking, the IMUs on the legs provide the necessary data to position the lower body correctly, even while the Aria glasses are looking forward at a wall.

Vision-Assisted Calibration

One of the most tedious aspects of wearable motion capture is calibration. Traditionally, users must stand in a specific "T-pose" or "A-pose," and the system must know exactly how each sensor is oriented relative to the bone it is attached to. This is often brittle; if a sensor shifts slightly during a run, the data becomes inaccurate.

RoSHI introduces a vision-assisted calibration method that uses AprilTags (machine-readable barcodes). Each IMU has an AprilTag attached to its casing. To calibrate, the user simply records a 30-second video of themselves moving naturally using a smartphone.

Performance and Evaluation

The researchers evaluated RoSHI against several baselines, including a high-end OptiTrack motion capture system used as "ground truth." The evaluation focused on three datasets ranging from simple in-place motions to agile sports activities like tennis and ball-throwing.

Two primary metrics were used:

  • Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE): The average distance (in centimeters) between the predicted joints and the actual joints.
  • Joint Angle Error (JAE): The angular difference (in degrees) between the predicted bone directions and the ground truth.

Figure 2: Qualitative comparison of RoSHI against other methods. RoSHI maintains accurate poses and global positioning even during complex tasks like picking up boxes or playing tennis, where vision-only methods (SAM 3D) or IMU-only methods often fail.

The results showed that RoSHI consistently achieved the lowest MPJPE (averaging around 10 cm) across all datasets. While vision-only systems like SAM 3D Body performed well when the subject was clearly visible, their performance dropped significantly when the subject moved out of view or was occluded. RoSHI maintained high accuracy and data coverage because the wearable IMUs continue to function regardless of visual line-of-sight.

From Human Data to Humanoid Control

The ultimate test for RoSHI was whether the captured data could be used to control a real humanoid robot. The researchers retargeted the captured human motion onto a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

Because humans and robots have different physical structures (different limb lengths and joint limits), the motion must be "retargeted." The RoSHI data was processed through an optimization pipeline to ensure the robot's movements were physically possible while remaining faithful to the human demonstration. These retargeted motions were then used to train reinforcement learning (RL) policies in a simulation environment.

Figure 3: Deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid. The robot successfully performs diverse behaviors such as waving and carrying objects, learned directly from "in-the-wild" human data collected with RoSHI.

The trained policies were successfully deployed on the physical Unitree G1 robot. The robot was able to perform coordinated, whole-body movements including walking, bowing, and waving. Notably, the stable global trajectory provided by the Aria glasses' SLAM was critical for the robot to maintain its balance and direction over long sequences.

Conclusion

RoSHI represents a practical step toward scaling up the data available for humanoid robot learning. By refusing to choose between the portability of IMUs and the global consistency of vision, it provides a versatile tool for capturing how humans interact with the world. Its use of low-cost hardware and a simplified calibration routine makes it a viable option for researchers looking to collect large-scale datasets outside of laboratory environments. As humanoid robots become more common, systems like RoSHI will likely play a role in providing the diverse demonstrations needed for them to navigate and work in human-centric spaces.
