Have We Mastered Scale in Deep Monocular Visual SLAM? The ScaleMaster Dataset and Benchmark

HJ
Hyoseok Ju
BS
Bokeon Suh
GK
Giseop Kim

Abstract

Recent advances in deep monocular visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) have achieved impressive accuracy and dense reconstruction capabilities, yet their robustness to scale inconsistency in large-scale indoor environments remains largely unexplored. Existing benchmarks are limited to room-scale or structurally simple settings, leaving critical issues of intra-session scale drift and inter-session scale ambiguity insufficiently addressed. To fill this gap, we introduce the ScaleMaster Dataset, the first benchmark explicitly designed to evaluate scale consistency under challenging scenarios such as multi-floor structures, long trajectories, repetitive views, and low-texture regions. We systematically analyze the vulnerability of state-of-the-art deep monocular visual SLAM systems to scale inconsistency, providing both quantitative and qualitative evaluations. Crucially, our analysis extends beyond traditional trajectory metrics to include a direct map-to-map quality assessment using metrics like Chamfer distance against high-fidelity 3D ground truth. Our results reveal that while recent deep monocular visual SLAM systems demonstrate strong performance on existing benchmarks, they suffer from severe scale-related failures in realistic, large-scale indoor environments. By releasing the ScaleMaster dataset and baseline results, we aim to establish a foundation for future research toward developing scale-consistent and reliable visual SLAM systems.

AI Overview

Deep Monocular Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) systems have seen substantial progress with the advent of deep learning architectures that can estimate dense 3D structures and camera poses from a single camera stream. While modern systems demonstrate high accuracy on established room-scale datasets, their performance in complex, large-scale environments often degrades due to the fundamental problem of scale inconsistency. Because monocular cameras cannot observe absolute metric scale directly, these systems must estimate it, leading to drift and ambiguity. This report explores the ScaleMaster benchmark, which identifies and quantifies the limitations of current state-of-the-art deep SLAM systems in realistic, large-scale indoor scenarios.

The Challenge of Scale in Monocular SLAM

In monocular visual SLAM, the absolute depth of objects in a scene is unobservable from a single moving camera without prior information. While a system can determine the relative structure of the environment and the direction of its motion, the actual physical size—the scale factor sss—remains ambiguous. Deep learning models attempt to resolve this by learning priors about the size of common objects or by using geometric constraints across multiple frames. However, maintaining a consistent sss across an entire trajectory is a complex challenge.

Scale inconsistency can be broadly categorized into three types:

  1. Short-term intra-session scale drift: Rapid changes in the estimated scale factor sis_isi​ for a frame iii due to challenging visual conditions, such as repetitive patterns on a staircase or vertical motion through elevators.
  2. Long-term intra-session scale drift: The gradual accumulation of scale errors over hundreds of meters. This drift can prevent successful loop closure, as the system may arrive back at a known location but perceive it at a different scale, causing the pose-graph optimization to fail.
  3. Inter-session scale ambiguity: The inconsistency between independent mapping sessions. If a robot maps the same corridor twice, the two resulting maps might have different metric scales, making it impossible to merge them into a single global map without complex re-alignment.

Current benchmarks like TUM-RGBD or EuRoC primarily consist of room-scale environments where trajectories are short (often under 20 meters). In these constrained settings, scale drift is negligible or easily manageable. However, real-world robotic applications often require navigation through multi-floor buildings with corridors spanning hundreds of meters, where even small scale errors compound into catastrophic failures.

The ScaleMaster Dataset

To address the limitations of existing benchmarks, the ScaleMaster dataset provides 25 sequences captured in large, complex indoor environments. These environments include multi-floor structures, long corridors, and challenging architectural features like repetitive glass partitions and open halls.

Data Acquisition Hardware

The data was collected using a custom handheld rig designed to provide both high-quality visual data and precise geometric ground truth. The rig integrates:

  • Visual and Odometry Source: An iPhone 14 Pro utilizing the ARKit framework to capture high-resolution images and preliminary trajectory data.
  • LiDAR Sensor: A Livox HAP LiDAR for capturing dense, high-fidelity 3D point clouds of the environment.
  • Depth Camera: An Orbbec Gemini 335L camera for additional visual-depth data.

Figure 2: The hardware rig used for data collection and the pipeline for generating 3D ground truth reference maps.

Benchmark Characteristics

ScaleMaster differs from existing datasets in several dimensions:

  • Scale of Environment: Trajectories reach lengths of up to 900 meters, far exceeding the 5-15 meter range typical of previous benchmarks.
  • Complexity: Sequences specifically target "hard" cases for SLAM, such as vertical motion in stairwells and escalators, which often trigger scale "explosions" where the map grows or shrinks abnormally.
  • Ground Truth: For 7 of the most challenging sequences, the dataset provides dense LiDAR-based 3D maps. This allows for direct geometric evaluation of the SLAM system's output, rather than relying solely on the camera's trajectory.

Redefining SLAM Evaluation Metrics

The standard metric for evaluating SLAM is the Absolute Trajectory Error (ATEATEATE) RMSERMSERMSE. To calculate ATEATEATE in monocular SLAM, the estimated trajectory must be aligned to the ground truth using a similarity transformation Sim(3)Sim(3)Sim(3), which estimates the best-fit scale sss, rotation R\mathbf{R}R, and translation t\mathbf{t}t.

However, ATEATEATE can be misleading. A system might estimate a trajectory that "looks" correct on a 2D plot but produces a 3D map that is geometrically warped or collapsed. To solve this, the ScaleMaster benchmark introduces map-oriented metrics.

Analysis of State-of-the-Art Systems

The researchers evaluated three prominent deep SLAM systems: DROID-SLAM, MASt3R-SLAM, and VGGT-SLAM. These systems represent the current peak of monocular performance, utilizing dense pointmap matching and iterative bundle adjustment.

The ATE Paradox

The evaluation revealed a significant discrepancy between trajectory accuracy and map quality. In one sequence (Library 07), MASt3R-SLAM achieved a deceptively low ATEATEATE RMSERMSERMSE of 0.12 meters. However, the map evaluation revealed a Drop Rate of 89.1%, meaning almost 90% of the reconstructed points were outliers. This indicates that while the camera's path appeared correct, the 3D map had suffered a scale collapse, rendering it useless for navigation or interaction.

Figure 3: Qualitative comparison of two sequences. Despite similar camera motion, Library 06 (bottom) shows a successful reconstruction, while Library 07 (top) exhibits catastrophic map failure undetected by trajectory metrics.

Failure Modes in Complex Environments

The benchmark highlights specific scenarios where these systems struggle:

  • Vertical Motion: Sequences like Stairs 01 involve repetitive visual patterns. As the user ascends, the SLAM system often fails to associate the current stairs with the previous ones correctly, leading to a scale explosion where the estimated height of the stairs increases exponentially.
  • Large Halls: In sequences like LargeHall 01, the lack of nearby visual features and the long trajectory lengths lead to ATEATEATE values as high as 80-90 meters for systems that otherwise perform well in smaller rooms.
  • Inter-session Inconsistency: When the same sequence is processed in multiple chunks (sessions), the resulting map fragments often have wildly different scales. This prevents systems from reliably building a global map through collaborative or incremental mapping.

Conclusion and Future Directions

The ScaleMaster benchmark demonstrates that "mastering" scale in monocular SLAM remains an unsolved problem for large-scale indoor environments. While deep learning models can generalize well to small environments, they are vulnerable to drift and scale collapse when faced with the complexities of real-world buildings.

The findings suggest that the SLAM community must move beyond trajectory-centric metrics. The inclusion of the Chamfer distance and Drop Rate as primary evaluation criteria provides a more truthful representation of a system's utility for robotics. Future work in the field will likely need to focus on:

  1. Robust scale priors: Developing models that can maintain a stable metric scale over kilometer-scale distances.
  2. Scale-aware optimization: Improving loop closure and bundle adjustment algorithms to specifically handle scale drift in the pose-graph.
  3. Better feature association: Enhancing the ability of systems to handle repetitive structures and vertical motion without triggering scale explosions.

By providing a rigorous testing ground in the form of the ScaleMaster dataset, this research establishes a new standard for robustness, pushing monocular SLAM toward reliable deployment in large-scale autonomous systems.
