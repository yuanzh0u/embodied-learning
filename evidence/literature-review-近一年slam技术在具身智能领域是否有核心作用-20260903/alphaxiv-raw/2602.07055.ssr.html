Theory of Space: Can Foundation Models Construct Spatial Beliefs through Active Exploration?

Abstract

Spatial embodied intelligence requires agents to act to acquire information under partial observability. While multimodal foundation models excel at passive perception, their capacity for active, self-directed exploration remains understudied. We propose Theory of Space, defined as an agent's ability to actively acquire information through self-directed, active exploration and to construct, revise, and exploit a spatial belief from sequential, partial observations. We evaluate this through a benchmark where the goal is curiosity-driven exploration to build an accurate cognitive map. A key innovation is spatial belief probing, which prompts models to reveal their internal spatial representations at each step. Our evaluation of state-of-the-art models reveals several critical bottlenecks. First, we identify an Active-Passive Gap, where performance drops significantly when agents must autonomously gather information. Second, we find high inefficiency, as models explore unsystematically compared to program-based proxies. Through belief probing, we diagnose that while perception is an initial bottleneck, global beliefs suffer from instability that causes spatial knowledge to degrade over time. Finally, using a false belief paradigm, we uncover Belief Inertia, where agents fail to update obsolete priors with new evidence. This issue is present in text-based agents but is particularly severe in vision-based models. Our findings suggest that current foundation models struggle to maintain coherent, revisable spatial beliefs during active exploration.

AI Overview

Problem Context and Motivation

The development of embodied artificial intelligence has reached a critical juncture where foundation models demonstrate impressive capabilities in passive reasoning tasks but struggle when required to actively explore and understand physical environments. This paper introduces "THEORY OF SPACE," a comprehensive framework designed to evaluate whether multimodal foundation models (MFMs) can construct coherent spatial beliefs through autonomous exploration.

The research addresses a fundamental gap in current AI evaluation: most existing benchmarks assess models on pre-collected, static observations or goal-specific navigation tasks. However, true spatial intelligence requires the ability to actively decide "what to observe next" to build a general understanding of the environment, independent of specific objectives. This capability mirrors human spatial cognition, where active exploration leads to superior spatial understanding compared to passive observation.

The authors draw an analogy to Theory of Mind (ToM), which evaluates an agent's ability to model hidden mental states of others. Similarly, THEORY OF SPACE assesses an agent's capacity to model the hidden physical structure of the world, moving beyond task completion metrics to examine the internal mechanisms of spatial cognition.

Framework Architecture

The THEORY OF SPACE framework operates on three fundamental operations that define spatial intelligence:

Construct: The ability to form a globally consistent internal spatial belief from sequential, partial observations through active exploration. This involves integrating local observations into a coherent global representation while maintaining spatial relationships and object properties.

Revise: The dynamic updating of internal beliefs to resolve conflicts with new information, particularly when environmental changes occur. This requires detecting inconsistencies and adapting existing spatial models accordingly.

Exploit: The utilization of current spatial beliefs to support various downstream tasks, demonstrating that the constructed representation is useful for practical applications.

The framework implements these operations through a novel assessment paradigm with three essential components:

Task-Agnostic Active Exploration

Unlike traditional benchmarks that guide agents toward specific goals, this framework requires agents to autonomously select actions (move, rotate, observe, query, terminate) to gather information and build a general-purpose internal model. The exploration is decoupled from any downstream task, focusing purely on understanding the spatial structure.

The framework includes a "False Belief" paradigm where the environment is subtly altered after initial exploration. This tests the agent's ability to detect changes and revise its spatial belief, mirroring cognitive science experiments that assess belief revision capabilities.

Belief Exploitation Assessment

Drawing from cognitive science literature, the framework evaluates two types of spatial knowledge:

  • Belief on Route: Assesses path-based, egocentric understanding through tasks like pairwise spatial relationships, perspective-taking, and action-to-view mapping
  • Belief on Survey: Evaluates map-like, allocentric understanding through allocentric mapping, mental rotation, and view-to-location tasks

The assessment uses open-ended questions requiring structured answers to prevent knowledge leakage, with scoring based on correctness components such as direction and distance accuracy.

Explicit Belief Probing

The most innovative aspect of the framework is its explicit probing of internal spatial beliefs:

Cognitive Map Probing: Agents must externalize their evolving spatial belief as a structured JSON-formatted cognitive map at each exploration step. This map includes positions and orientations of observed objects, evaluated across multiple metrics:

  • Correctness (positional, directional, facing accuracy)
  • Perception (interpreting new local observations)
  • Self-tracking (agent's own pose estimation)
  • Local↔Global consistency (integrating local evidence into global belief)
  • Stability (maintaining beliefs about previously observed objects)

Uncertainty Map Probing: Agents identify unobserved regions in a top-down view, revealing their uncertainty modeling capabilities through F1 score evaluation.

Experimental Design

The benchmark implementation creates controlled environments for systematic evaluation:

Environment Setup

Procedurally generated multi-room indoor layouts on N × M grids populated with indoor objects, each having 2D coordinates and cardinal orientations. Two modalities are supported:

  • Text-based World: Provides symbolic observations with discretized egocentric direction and distance bins (e.g., "chair is front-left and near"), isolating pure spatial reasoning
  • Vision-based World: Provides egocentric RGB images rendered in ThreeDWorld with Objaverse assets, requiring visual perception to recover spatial relations

Action Space and Evaluation

The action space includes high-level actions: Goto (to visible objects), Rotate (90°, 180°, 270°), Observe (perceive 90° field of view), Query (get absolute coordinates), and Terminate exploration. Each action has associated costs to encourage efficient exploration.

Key Experimental Findings

The evaluation of state-of-the-art foundation models revealed several critical insights:

The Active-Passive Performance Gap

A significant finding is the substantial performance degradation when models must actively explore versus passively receiving observations. For instance, GPT-5.2's average accuracy dropped from 57.1% (passive) to 46.0% (active) in vision-based environments, while GEMINI-3 PRO showed a decrease from 60.5% to 57.3%.

Active exploration proved inefficient, with models requiring ≥14 steps to achieve coverage that program-based proxies accomplished in ~9 steps. Models frequently terminated exploration prematurely, leaving significant spatial uncertainty unresolved.

Modality-Specific Challenges

Text-based environments consistently yielded higher performance than vision-based environments across all models. This indicates that grounding spatial concepts from raw visual input remains a significant challenge for current MFMs, with humans maintaining superior performance in both modalities but showing an even greater advantage in vision-based tasks.

Cognitive Map Instability

The explicit belief probing revealed critical weaknesses in spatial memory:

  • Perception Bottlenecks: Vision-based models struggled particularly with object orientation inference, showing near-chance performance on facing direction identification
  • Belief Decay: Even when objects were initially perceived correctly, spatial beliefs about these objects degraded over time, indicating fragile state maintenance
  • Integration Failures: Models struggled to coherently integrate new local evidence into stable, consistent global spatial maps

Belief Inertia in Dynamic Environments

When environments changed, models exhibited "spatial belief inertia," persisting with obsolete spatial priors despite direct observation of new configurations. This was particularly severe in vision-based agents, especially for object orientation updates. Models also engaged in excessive redundant exploration after environmental changes, failing to efficiently identify when belief updating was complete.

Contributions and Implications

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of embodied AI and spatial reasoning:

Diagnostic Framework Innovation

The explicit cognitive map and uncertainty map probing mechanisms provide unprecedented diagnostic capabilities, moving beyond black-box behavioral assessment to white-box representational analysis. This allows researchers to identify specific failure modes in spatial reasoning rather than merely detecting performance deficits.

Identification of Fundamental Limitations

The work empirically demonstrates that current foundation models, despite impressive reasoning capabilities, suffer from critical weaknesses in autonomous information gathering and spatial belief maintenance. The identified "active-passive gap" represents a major bottleneck for developing truly embodied intelligence.

Research Direction Guidance

The findings highlight several crucial areas for future research:

  • Development of uncertainty-aware exploration policies
  • Enhanced state maintenance mechanisms for stable spatial beliefs
  • Improved belief revision systems that can overcome spatial inertia
  • Better integration of visual perception with spatial reasoning

The THEORY OF SPACE framework establishes a new paradigm for evaluating embodied AI that emphasizes the internal mechanisms of spatial cognition rather than just task completion. By providing both a conceptual framework and rigorous diagnostic tools, this work is positioned to drive research toward more intelligent and adaptable spatial agents capable of genuine environmental understanding.

Movement-produced stimulation in the development of visually guided behavior
This foundational cognitive science paper provides the core motivation for the study, establishing that active exploration leads to substantially better spatial understanding than passively receiving the same information. The main paper's 'Active-Passive Gap' is a direct test of this principle in foundation models.
Richard Held and Alan Hein. Movement-produced stimulation in the development of visually guided behavior. Journal of comparative and physiological psychology, 56(5):872, 1963.
Cognitive maps in rats and men
This is the seminal paper that introduced the concept of 'cognitive maps.' This concept is central to the main paper's methodology, which uses the explicit probing of an agent's internal cognitive map as a key technique for evaluating its spatial belief.
Edward C Tolman. Cognitive maps in rats and men. Psychological review, 55(4):189, 1948.
The development of spatial representations of large-scale environments
This work is directly cited to justify the design of the downstream evaluation tasks. The paper's distinction between 'Belief on Route' and 'Belief on Survey' knowledge is adapted from the spatial representation framework developed in this classic paper.
Alan W. Siegel and Sheldon H. White. The development of spatial representations of large-scale environments. Advances in Child Development and Behavior, 10:9–55, 1975.
Beliefs about beliefs: Representation and constraining function of wrong beliefs in young children’s understanding of deception

Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner. Beliefs about beliefs: Representation and constraining function of wrong beliefs in young children’s understanding of deception. Cognition, 13(1):103–128, 1983.
Alfred: A benchmark for interpreting grounded instructions for everyday tasks
This citation represents the class of task-driven active exploration benchmarks that the THEORY OF SPACE paper contrasts itself with. Citing a major benchmark like ALFRED helps situate the paper's contribution of task-agnostic, curiosity-driven exploration within the broader landscape of embodied AI research.
