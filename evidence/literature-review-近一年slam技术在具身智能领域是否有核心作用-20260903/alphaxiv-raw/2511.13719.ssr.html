Abstract

Despite remarkable progress, multimodal foundation models still exhibit surprising deficiencies in spatial intelligence. In this work, we explore scaling up multimodal foundation models to cultivate spatial intelligence within the SenseNova-SI family, built upon established multimodal foundations including visual understanding models (i.e., Qwen3-VL and InternVL3) and unified understanding and generation models (i.e., Bagel). We take a principled approach to constructing high-performing and robust spatial intelligence by systematically curating SenseNova-SI-8M: eight million diverse data samples under a rigorous taxonomy of spatial capabilities. SenseNova-SI demonstrates unprecedented performance across a broad range of spatial intelligence benchmarks: 68.8% on VSI-Bench, 43.3% on MMSI, 85.7% on MindCube, 54.7% on ViewSpatial, 47.7% on SITE, 63.9% on BLINK, 55.5% on 3DSR, and 72.0% on EmbSpatial, while maintaining strong general multimodal understanding (e.g., 84.9% on MMBench-En). More importantly, we analyze the impact of data scaling, discuss early signs of emergent generalization capabilities enabled by diverse data training, analyze the risk of overfitting and language shortcuts, present a preliminary study on spatial chain-of-thought reasoning, and validate the potential downstream application. All newly trained multimodal foundation models are publicly released.

AI Overview

The Challenge of Spatial Intelligence in Foundation Models

While multimodal foundation models (MFMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities across various general tasks, their performance in spatial intelligence (SI) remains a persistent challenge. Spatial intelligence is defined as the ability to comprehend, reason about, and interact within three-dimensional space. This capability is foundational for moving from general image understanding toward embodied Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), where an agent must navigate and manipulate objects in the physical world.

The current research landscape identifies a significant gap between human spatial reasoning and model performance. High-quality, spatially grounded data is often scarce or fragmented, hindering the development of robust SI. Even highly capable proprietary models have been shown to exhibit notable deficiencies in spatial tasks, with performance gaps between open-source and closed-source models being relatively small in this domain compared to general language tasks.

Previous attempts to improve SI in MFMs have typically followed two paths: integrating specialized 3D expert encoders to derive geometric attributes or curating spatial-specific datasets for fine-tuning. However, these datasets often disproportionately cover certain spatial capabilities while underrepresenting critical areas such as perspective-taking and 3D mental reconstruction. This work addresses these limitations by adopting a data-centric strategy, focusing on scaling and diversifying training data rather than modifying existing MFM architectures.

A Data-Centric Taxonomy for Spatial Reasoning

To systematically improve spatial intelligence, this research employs a principled taxonomy to guide data curation. This taxonomy decomposes spatial intelligence into five core dimensions, providing a comprehensive framework for evaluation and training.

  1. Metric Measurement (MM): This dimension focuses on the understanding of physical scale and distance. It involves tasks such as estimating the distance between the camera and an object, calculating distances between two objects, and estimating the physical size of objects or entire scenes.
  2. Spatial Relations (SR): This involves reasoning within a 3D coordinate system (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z). It covers egocentric relations (such as front-back, left-right, and up-down from the observer's perspective) as well as global scene-level relations (near-far and relative scale).
  3. Mental Reconstruction (MR): This capability requires a model to infer 3D structure from limited 2D visual inputs. An example task is identifying which side of an object is visible based on a single image.
  4. Perspective-Taking (PT): This is a hierarchical set of tasks addressing reasoning with changing camera viewpoints. It includes:
    • View Correspondence: Establishing object or point correspondences across different views despite changes in viewpoint, scale, or occlusion.
    • Camera Motion Reasoning: Inferring relative camera motion between views based on visual changes.
    • Allocentric Transformation: Simulating viewpoint shifts and expressing spatial relations across various coordinate systems (camera-oriented, object-oriented, or self-oriented).
  5. Comprehensive Reasoning (CR): This involves integrating multiple spatial capabilities with extended memory and multi-step reasoning.

By organizing data according to these dimensions, the researchers aim to ensure that models develop a holistic understanding of 3D space rather than just memorizing specific spatial labels.

Scaling Spatial Intelligence: The SenseNova-SI-8M Dataset

The core of this research is the construction of SenseNova-SI-8M, a large-scale dataset comprising eight million diverse data samples. This dataset is designed to address the fragmentation and scarcity of existing spatial data.

The SenseNova-SI-8M corpus is derived from three main sources:

  • General Question-Answering (0.6M pairs): This includes established 2D image understanding datasets to maintain general vision-language alignment.
  • Community Spatial Intelligence Datasets (3.3M pairs): This aggregates existing spatial datasets, though they are often heterogeneous and concentrated on specific tasks.
  • Scaled Spatial Intelligence Data (4.5M pairs): To address gaps in underrepresented areas like perspective-taking and mental reconstruction, the researchers generated 4.5 million additional pairs. This involved using richly annotated 3D datasets such as ScanNet and Ego-Exo4D.

The data generation pipeline for the scaled portion of the dataset involves unified annotation and a frame selection algorithm that controls for visual overlap. This ensures that the generated questions are high-quality, reduce ambiguity, and provide a balanced sampling of spatial tasks. The result is a diverse training set that covers the entire taxonomy of spatial capabilities.

Methodology and Model Architectures

The methodology focuses on leveraging existing, powerful generalist MFMs and fine-tuning them on the curated spatial data. The study utilizes three established model families as backbones:

  • Qwen3-VL: A model that expands a language foundation to incorporate vision and audio modalities.
  • InternVL-3: A model designed for native multimodality with joint vision-language training.
  • Bagel: A model representing a unified understanding and generation paradigm.

Each model was fine-tuned for one epoch on the SenseNova-SI-8M dataset. Training was conducted using 128 GPUs with a batch size of 2048. The optimization process used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5×10−65 \times 10^{-6}5×10−6. For video data, the models sampled up to 16 frames per instance, ensuring a consistent context for temporal and spatial reasoning.

The models, collectively called SenseNova-SI, were then evaluated across a comprehensive suite of spatial intelligence benchmarks (including VSI-Bench, MMSI-Bench, and MindCube) as well as general multimodal benchmarks (like MMBench and MMMU) to ensure that the spatial training did not lead to catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities.

Empirical Findings and Scaling Laws

The research provides insights into how spatial intelligence scales with data and model size. The findings demonstrate that data scaling leads to consistent improvements across all primary spatial dimensions.

One notable observation is that data mixing—aggregating diverse public datasets with newly curated samples—allows smaller models to achieve significant performance gains. For instance, the SenseNova-SI-2B model outperformed some 7B-parameter models that were trained with comparable data budgets but lacked the same diversity. This suggests that for spatial intelligence, the quality and breadth of data are just as important as the number of parameters.

However, distinct scaling behaviors were observed across model sizes. Smaller models exhibited limitations in learning complex capabilities like viewpoint transformations, whereas larger models showed a more robust ability to handle allocentric reasoning. While performance gains appeared to diminish at very high data volumes, indicating potential saturation, the study highlighted "capability synergy." Advances in fundamental tasks, such as basic spatial relations, were found to transfer to more complex reasoning skills, suggesting that a strong foundation in simple spatial tasks is necessary for high-level spatial intelligence.

The models also demonstrated "extrapolation" capabilities. Although they were trained with a maximum of 16 frames per sample, the SenseNova-SI models generalized effectively to sequences of 32 or more frames during inference. This indicates that the models acquired a coherent internal representation of spatial structure that transcends the specific frame limits of the training set.

Robustness and the Limitations of Textual Reasoning

A critical aspect of the research was validating whether the models were genuinely performing spatial reasoning or merely relying on textual shortcuts and biases.

To test this, the researchers used debiased benchmarks and "circular tests," where the order of multiple-choice answers is shuffled. SenseNova-SI exhibited a smaller performance drop compared to other models, indicating a reduced dependence on textual heuristics. Furthermore, in experiments where visual inputs were removed, SenseNova-SI's performance significantly declined, validating that the model was indeed grounded in the visual data. This contrasts with some other open-source models whose performance remained stable without images, suggesting they were relying on language patterns rather than spatial understanding.

The study also investigated the effectiveness of text-based Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for spatial tasks. While CoT has been highly successful in mathematical and logical reasoning, its benefits for spatial intelligence were found to be limited. Long textual CoT chains were prone to internal inconsistencies and errors when describing complex 3D scenes. These findings suggest that text-based reasoning may not be the most efficient paradigm for spatial intelligence, and alternative mechanisms—perhaps involving latent geometric representations—might be required for future advancements.

Generalization to Embodied AI and Robotic Tasks

To demonstrate the practical utility of enhanced spatial intelligence, the researchers evaluated SenseNova-SI on robotic manipulation tasks using the EmbodiedBench benchmark. In these tests, the model acted as an embodied agent controlling a simulated Franka Panda robot.

Notably, no fine-tuning was performed for these robotic applications. Despite this, SenseNova-SI-InternVL3-8B showed substantial increases in success rates compared to its base model. The success rate improved by approximately 60% across different prompting strategies. This indicates that the spatial intelligence gained from the SenseNova-SI-8M dataset directly translates to practical applications, enabling more reliable object identification, accurate spatial reasoning, and consistent action planning in physical environments.

Conclusion and Implications

The SenseNova-SI series and the associated research represent a significant step in addressing the spatial intelligence gap in multimodal foundation models. By shifting the focus toward a systematic, data-centric scaling strategy guided by a rigorous taxonomy, the work demonstrates that robust spatial reasoning is achievable using existing architectures.

The findings challenge the universal effectiveness of textual Chain-of-Thought for spatial reasoning and highlight the importance of data diversity in preventing reliance on textual shortcuts. Furthermore, the demonstrated success in zero-shot robotic manipulation underscores the potential of these models as foundational components for embodied AI. By releasing the models and the training framework, the researchers provide a platform for the broader community to explore the next generation of spatially aware artificial intelligence.
