Abstract

We present Online3R, a new sequential reconstruction framework that is capable of adapting to new scenes through online learning, effectively resolving inconsistency issues. Specifically, we introduce a set of learnable lightweight visual prompts into a pretrained, frozen geometry foundation model to capture the knowledge of new environments while preserving the fundamental capability of the foundation model for geometry prediction. To solve the problems of missing groundtruth and the requirement of high efficiency when updating these visual prompts at test time, we introduce a local-global self-supervised learning strategy by enforcing the local and global consistency constraints on predictions. The local consistency constraints are conducted on intermediate and previously local fused results, enabling the model to be trained with high-quality pseudo groundtruth signals; the global consistency constraints are operated on sparse keyframes spanning long distances rather than per frame, allowing the model to learn from a consistent prediction over a long trajectory in an efficient way. Our experiments demonstrate that Online3R outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods on various benchmarks. Project page: this https URL

AI Overview

In recent years, 3D reconstruction from multi-view images has shifted from traditional geometric optimization toward deep learning-based geometry foundation models. These models, such as MASt3R and DUSt3R, can directly infer 3D pointmaps and camera poses from pairs of images. However, when these models are used for sequential reconstruction—building a map from a video stream—they often struggle with "drift" over time and lack the ability to adapt to specific features of a new, unseen environment.

The research presented in Online3R addresses this by introducing an online learning framework that allows a frozen geometry foundation model to adapt to specific scenes during inference. By integrating parameter-efficient visual prompt tuning with self-supervised consistency constraints, the system achieves higher geometric consistency and camera pose accuracy than static models without requiring offline fine-tuning or ground-truth 3D data.

Figure 1: The Online3R framework. It introduces learnable visual prompts into a frozen foundation model (MASt3R) and updates them online using local and global consistency losses derived from streaming data.

The Challenge of Sequential Reconstruction

Sequential 3D reconstruction involves Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), where a system must estimate its own position while simultaneously building a map of its surroundings. Geometry foundation models have simplified this by providing dense pointmaps directly from image pairs. However, these models are typically "frozen" after training. While they generalize well across different datasets, they cannot capture scene-specific priors that would make the reconstruction more consistent.

Existing methods for sequential reconstruction usually follow one of two paths: they either use models specifically designed for temporal sequences (which often lack global consistency) or they use refinement techniques like graph optimization on top of a frozen model's predictions. The primary limitation remains: a universal model may not perform optimally in every environment. Online3R proposes that instead of a better universal model, we need a mechanism for models to learn and adapt "on the fly" as they encounter new environments.

Visual Prompt Tuning for 3D Foundation Models

To enable adaptation without the prohibitive cost of updating millions of neural network parameters, Online3R employs Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT). Instead of retraining the entire model, the framework inserts a small set of learnable parameters, called "prompts," into the encoder layers of the network.

In the context of a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone like the one used in MASt3R, the input image is divided into tokens. These image tokens are then concatenated with a set of NpN_pNp​ learnable prompt tokens, each having a dimension DDD. As the tokens pass through the self-attention layers of the encoder, the prompt tokens interact with the image tokens. This interaction allows the prompts to modulate the feature extraction process based on what the system has learned about the current scene.

Formally, the prompts are represented as a set of tokens:

Pt={p1,p2,…,pNp}∈RNp×DP_t = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_{N_p}\} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times D}Pt​={p1​,p2​,…,pNp​​}∈RNp​×D

These prompts are the only parameters updated during the online learning process. The core weights of the underlying foundation model remain untouched, preserving the robust geometric knowledge learned during its large-scale pre-training.

Self-Supervised Consistency Constraints

Since ground-truth 3D maps are not available during real-time operation, Online3R uses a self-supervised approach to update the prompts. This is achieved through two primary constraints: local and global consistency.

Local Consistency

Local consistency ensures that the model's immediate predictions align with the integrated knowledge gathered from previous frames. In sequential reconstruction, systems often maintain a "fused" pointmap for each keyframe. This fused map, denoted as X~l−1l−1\tilde{X}_{l-1}^{l-1}X~l−1l−1​ for a keyframe Kl−1K_{l-1}Kl−1​, is created by averaging the geometric predictions from several subsequent views. Because it combines information from multiple perspectives, the fused map is typically more accurate than any single prediction.

This loss forces the prompt-modulated network to reproduce the high-quality, fused geometry, effectively "distilling" the temporal information back into the network's parameters.

Global Consistency

To prevent the model from drifting over long trajectories or overfitting to recent frames, the system enforces global consistency. This constraint requires that the geometry of a specific keyframe KlK_lKl​ should look the same regardless of which historical keyframe it is compared against.

When a new keyframe is processed, the system samples two random historical keyframes Kh1K_{h1}Kh1​ and Kh2K_{h2}Kh2​ from its memory (the pose graph). It then performs two separate forward passes to predict the pointmap for KlK_lKl​ relative to each historical frame. The global consistency loss Lglobal\mathcal{L}_{global}Lglobal​ is the difference between these two predictions:

This encourages the prompts to capture a globally coherent understanding of the scene geometry that is robust to changes in viewpoint.

System Implementation and Workflow

The Online3R framework operates on a monocular RGB video stream. It begins by using the base MASt3R-SLAM system to process incoming frames. As the camera moves, the system designates certain frames as "keyframes."

  1. Initialization: The prompts PtP_tPt​ are initialized with zeros or learned values from a previous sequence.
  2. Sequential Processing: For each new frame, the system estimates the camera pose by matching it against the most recent keyframe.
  3. Keyframe Update: When a new keyframe KlK_lKl​ is selected, the pointmaps are updated through a confidence-weighted averaging filter.
  4. Online Learning: Triggered by the new keyframe, the system calculates Llocal\mathcal{L}_{local}Llocal​ and Lglobal\mathcal{L}_{global}Lglobal​. The total loss Ltotal\mathcal{L}_{total}Ltotal​ is used to update the prompts:
    Ltotal=λLlocal+(1−λ)Lglobal\mathcal{L}_{total} = \lambda \mathcal{L}_{local} + (1 - \lambda) \mathcal{L}_{global}Ltotal​=λLlocal​+(1−λ)Lglobal​
    where λ\lambdaλ is typically set to 0.5.
  5. Prompt Update: The system uses the AdamW optimizer to adjust the prompt parameters PtP_tPt​. This entire process happens in the background while the system continues to process the video stream.

Evaluation and Results

The researchers evaluated Online3R across several standard datasets, including TUM RGB-D, NRGBD, and 7-Scenes. The evaluation focused on two main metrics: Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) for camera pose accuracy and geometric Accuracy/Completion for the quality of the 3D map.

Camera Pose Estimation

On the TUM RGB-D benchmark, Online3R achieved an average ATE of 0.027 meters in the calibrated setting, outperforming the baseline MASt3R-SLAM (0.030 m) and DROID-SLAM (0.038 m). In the uncalibrated setting—where camera intrinsics are unknown—Online3R* still maintained a competitive ATE of 0.056 m.

Dense Geometry Reconstruction

The impact of online adaptation was most evident in the reconstruction quality. On the 7-Scenes dataset, which contains challenging indoor environments, Online3R* achieved a geometric accuracy of 0.039 m. This was a significant improvement over the baseline MASt3R-SLAM* (0.068 m).

The qualitative results in Figure 3 demonstrate that the learned prompts allow the model to "hallucinate" consistent geometry for non-overlapping views. This is because the prompts act as a form of scene memory, encoding the priors of the environment that the frozen foundation model would otherwise ignore.

Figure 3: Visualization of reconstruction for non-overlapping image pairs. The prompts in Online3R enable the model to reconstruct scene-consistent geometry (e.g., monitor edges) that the original frozen model cannot resolve without direct overlap.

Efficiency and Trade-offs

A critical requirement for online learning is efficiency. The researchers found that Online3R* operates at approximately 10 Frames Per Second (FPS). While this is slightly slower than the baseline (13.2 FPS), it remains fast enough for many real-time robotics and AR/VR applications. This small decrease in speed is the trade-off for significantly higher reconstruction accuracy and consistency.

The ablation studies confirmed that both local and global consistency constraints are necessary. Removing the local constraint led to a drop in geometric accuracy (from 0.039 m to 0.044 m), while removing the global constraint similarly degraded performance. Together, they allow the system to refine local details while maintaining a cohesive global map.

Conclusion and Significance

Online3R demonstrates that geometry foundation models do not need to be static. By introducing a lightweight, online adaptation mechanism, the researchers have shown that these models can become significantly more accurate and consistent when tailored to a specific environment during inference.

This approach has broad implications for the future of SLAM and spatial computing. It provides a blueprint for how large, pre-trained "foundation" models can be deployed in the real world—not as rigid tools, but as adaptive systems that grow more capable as they explore an environment. For robotics, this means more reliable navigation in unknown spaces; for AR/VR, it means more stable and detailed digital representations of the physical world.
