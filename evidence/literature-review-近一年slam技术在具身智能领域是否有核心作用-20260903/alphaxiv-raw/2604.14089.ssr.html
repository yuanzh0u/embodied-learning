UMI-3D: Extending Universal Manipulation Interface from Vision-Limited to 3D Spatial Perception

ZW
Ziming Wang

Abstract

We present UMI-3D, a multimodal extension of the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) for robust and scalable data collection in embodied manipulation. While UMI enables portable, wrist-mounted data acquisition, its reliance on monocular visual SLAM makes it vulnerable to occlusions, dynamic scenes, and tracking failures, limiting its applicability in real-world environments. UMI-3D addresses these limitations by introducing a lightweight and low-cost LiDAR sensor tightly integrated into the wrist-mounted interface, enabling LiDAR-centric SLAM with accurate metric-scale pose estimation under challenging conditions. We further develop a hardware-synchronized multimodal sensing pipeline and a unified spatiotemporal calibration framework that aligns visual observations with LiDAR point clouds, producing consistent 3D representations of demonstrations. Despite maintaining the original 2D visuomotor policy formulation, UMI-3D significantly improves the quality and reliability of collected data, which directly translates into enhanced policy performance. Extensive real-world experiments demonstrate that UMI-3D not only achieves high success rates on standard manipulation tasks, but also enables learning of tasks that are challenging or infeasible for the original vision-only UMI setup, including large deformable object manipulation and articulated object operation. The system supports an end-to-end pipeline for data acquisition, alignment, training, and deployment, while preserving the portability and accessibility of the original UMI. All hardware and software components are open-sourced to facilitate large-scale data collection and accelerate research in embodied intelligence: this https URL.

AI Overview

Enhancing Robot Learning with Robust 3D Perception

The acquisition of high-quality, scalable demonstration data is a central challenge in training generalizable robot manipulation policies. Recent developments in the field have highlighted that the diversity and scale of datasets often dictate the performance of embodied intelligence, much like the scaling laws observed in large language models. The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) framework was established to address this by providing a low-cost, handheld gripper capable of recording human demonstrations that can be directly mapped to robot actions. However, the original UMI relied primarily on monocular visual SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping), which encounters significant failures in environments with poor lighting, low texture, or frequent occlusions.

Figure 1: The UMI-3D framework integrates a handheld multimodal sensor suite to collect robust, metric-consistent demonstration data for diffusion-based policy training.

UMI-3D extends this paradigm by integrating a lightweight LiDAR sensor into the wrist-mounted interface. This shift from vision-limited to 3D spatial perception aims to provide superior robustness in pose estimation and scene reconstruction. By grounding the data collection process in metric-scale geometry, UMI-3D enables the capture of complex interactions that were previously difficult for vision-only systems, such as manipulating large deformable objects or operating in featureless indoor spaces.

Limitations of Vision-Only Systems

The primary motivation for UMI-3D stems from the inherent vulnerabilities of monocular visual SLAM. Standard visual SLAM algorithms depend on tracking stable visual features across consecutive image frames. In real-world manipulation, several factors frequently break this tracking:

  1. Textureless Environments: Uniformly colored surfaces (e.g., white walls or stainless steel tables) do not provide enough distinct features for reliable tracking.
  2. Dynamic Occlusions: When a human demonstrator moves a large object, such as a box or a curtain, the object may block the camera's view of the static environment, causing the SLAM system to lose its global reference.
  3. Illumination Changes: Drastic shifts in lighting, such as backlighting from a window or shadows cast during movement, can cause feature detection to fail.
  4. Scale Ambiguity: Monocular cameras cannot inherently perceive absolute depth, often requiring external markers or side-mirrors to approximate scale, which complicates the setup and reduces accuracy.

These failures create a bottleneck for scalable robot learning. If data collection is restricted to "vision-friendly" environments, the resulting policies will lack the robustness needed for general-purpose deployment. UMI-3D addresses this by making the pose estimation system geometry-aware, ensuring that metric-consistent and temporally aligned data can be collected regardless of the visual complexity of the scene.

Hardware Design and Sensor Fusion

To maintain the portability of the original UMI while enhancing its perception capabilities, UMI-3D utilizes a custom-designed handheld gripper. The system consists of a 3D-printed parallel-jaw gripper, a LiDAR sensor, an industrial CMOS camera, and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU).

The sensor suite is mounted at the wrist to ensure a tight coupling between perception and the tool center point. Specifically, the system uses a Livox MID-360 LiDAR and a Hikrobot industrial camera equipped with a 185∘185^\circ185∘ fisheye lens. This ultra-wide field of view (FoV) is critical for maintaining visual context during rapid movements. Unlike consumer-grade cameras used in earlier versions, the industrial camera allows for precise hardware-level synchronization.

Figure 2: The hardware architecture features a central STM32 microcontroller for precise temporal synchronization between the LiDAR, camera, and IMU.

Temporal synchronization is a critical requirement for multimodal sensing. UMI-3D employs an STM32 microcontroller as a central clock source. It generates a 1 Hz1 \text{ Hz}1 Hz pulse-per-second (PPS) signal and a pseudo-GPRMC message to synchronize the LiDAR internal clock, while simultaneously providing a 20 Hz20 \text{ Hz}20 Hz trigger signal for the camera. This ensure that every visual frame is precisely timestamped relative to the LiDAR point clouds and IMU readings, preventing temporal drift that would otherwise corrupt the recorded trajectories. The total weight of the gripper is approximately 1120 g1120 \text{ g}1120 g, keeping it light enough for extended human use.

LiDAR-Inertial SLAM and Spatiotemporal Calibration

The core of the UMI-3D processing pipeline is its robust SLAM system, which fuses data from the LiDAR and IMU to estimate the sensor's trajectory. This is achieved using an Iterated Error-State Kalman Filter (ESIKF) operating on differentiable manifolds.

The system state is defined on a manifold that includes the IMU orientation, position, velocity, sensor biases, gravity, and the extrinsic transformation between the IMU and the LiDAR. We can represent the full state X\mathcal{X}X as:

where R∈SO(3)R \in SO(3)R∈SO(3) and p∈R3p \in \mathbb{R}^3p∈R3 represent the rotation and position of the sensor in the global frame. The ESIKF performs high-frequency state prediction using IMU measurements and corrects these predictions using LiDAR points. To ensure robust data association, LiDAR points are registered against a voxelized probabilistic map, using point-to-plane residuals to minimize drift.

Data Pipeline for Diffusion Policies

Once a demonstration is recorded, the raw sensor streams are processed into a format suitable for policy learning. UMI-3D adopts a diffusion-based policy framework, which models the distribution of future actions conditioned on visual observations.

  1. Alignment: Each LiDAR-derived pose is matched to the nearest visual frame using the synchronized timestamps.
  2. Action Representation: The policy predicts sequences of end-effector poses. To improve robustness, these are represented as relative transformations to the current gripper pose. This means the policy learns to predict how to move relative to its current state, rather than predicting absolute coordinates in a global room frame.
  3. Proprioception: The history of the end-effector's relative trajectory is provided as an input to the policy, implicitly giving the model information about the current velocity and direction of motion.

By using the robust trajectory from the LiDAR-Inertial SLAM as the ground-truth "action" in the training data, the resulting policy becomes much more reliable. Interestingly, while LiDAR is used to generate the high-quality training labels, the final policy can be trained to use only RGB images as input during inference, allowing for deployment on robots that might only have a camera.

Experimental Validation and Task Performance

The researchers evaluated UMI-3D across several challenging manipulation tasks designed to test the limits of vision-only systems.

Robustness to Environmental Conditions

One of the most striking results is the system's performance in "vision-hostile" environments. In a textureless white-wall environment, where traditional visual SLAM would immediately lose track, UMI-3D maintained accurate pose estimation by relying on the geometric structure of the room captured by the LiDAR. Similarly, during a curtain-pulling task, the system remained stable despite significant illumination changes and the large, non-rigid motion of the curtain fabric.

Figure 5: Comparison of SLAM stability in challenging conditions. UMI-3D maintains tracking on textureless walls and through dynamic occlusions where vision-only methods fail.

Task Evaluation

The system was tested on four primary tasks:

  • Cup Arrangement: A benchmark task involving picking up a cup and placing it on a saucer. The policy demonstrated significant generalization, succeeding with 64 different object combinations, including many unseen during training.
  • Curtain Pulling: This task highlights the ability to handle deformable objects. The policy achieved high success rates (up to 96%) even under extreme backlighting conditions.
  • Door Opening and Cup Placement: A long-horizon task requiring the robot to open a sliding door, retrieve a cup, and place it on a table. This task combined articulated object manipulation with precise placement.
  • Mouse Placement: A cross-embodiment test to ensure compatibility with existing datasets.

Figure 6: Snapshots of the diverse manipulation tasks performed by UMI-3D, including articulated door opening and deformable curtain manipulation.

Figure 7: The system generalizes across 64 different cup and saucer combinations, showing resilience to changes in object appearance.

Cross-Embodiment and Generalization

A significant advantage of UMI-3D is its compatibility with the broader UMI ecosystem. The researchers demonstrated that a policy trained on data from the original UMI (using a GoPro camera) could be deployed directly on the UMI-3D hardware (using the Hikrobot camera) for a mouse placement task without any retraining. This suggests that the visual observation spaces are sufficiently aligned, allowing for data sharing and joint training across different hardware generations.

Figure 8: Performance of a pre-trained policy on UMI-3D hardware. The system maintains high success rates when transferring policies across different camera setups.

UMI-3D establishes a more reliable foundation for robot learning by prioritizing geometric consistency. By solving the reliability issues of vision-only SLAM, it allows researchers to focus on scaling the complexity and variety of tasks. The open-source nature of the hardware and software further encourages the community to contribute to a growing library of metric-consistent robot demonstrations, moving closer to the goal of universal embodied intelligence.
