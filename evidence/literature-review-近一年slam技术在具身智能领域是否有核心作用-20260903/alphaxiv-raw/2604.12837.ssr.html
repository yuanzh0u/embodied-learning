Abstract

Visual SLAM algorithms achieve significant improvements through the exploration of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representations, particularly in generating high-fidelity dense maps. However, they depend on a static environment assumption and experience significant performance degradation in dynamic environments. This paper presents GGD-SLAM, a framework that employs a generalizable motion model to address the challenges of localization and dense mapping in dynamic environments - without predefined semantic annotations or depth input. Specifically, the proposed system employs a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) queue to manage incoming frames, facilitating dynamic semantic feature extraction through a sequential attention mechanism. This is integrated with a dynamic feature enhancer to separate static and dynamic components. Additionally, to minimize dynamic distractors' impact on the static components, we devise a method to fill occluded areas via static information sampling and design a distractor-adaptive Structure Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) loss tailored for dynamic environments, significantly enhancing the system's resilience. Experiments conducted on real-world dynamic datasets demonstrate that the proposed system achieves state-of-the-art performance in camera pose estimation and dense reconstruction in dynamic scenes.

AI Overview

Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (vSLAM) is a cornerstone of modern robotics, augmented reality (AR), and autonomous systems. It allows a device to determine its position (tracking) while simultaneously creating a map of its surroundings (mapping). In recent years, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a powerful representation for SLAM, enabling the creation of high-fidelity, photorealistic dense maps that far surpass the detail of traditional sparse point clouds.

However, a fundamental assumption in most SLAM systems is that the world is static. When these systems encounter moving objects—such as walking people, moving vehicles, or even objects being repositioned—their performance typically degrades. Dynamic objects introduce "ghosting" artifacts in maps and corrupt the geometric calculations used for camera tracking. GGD-SLAM addresses this challenge by introducing a Generalizable Motion Model (GMM) designed to identify and handle dynamic elements without requiring pre-defined labels or specialized sensors.

The Challenge of Dynamic Environments

The core difficulty in dynamic SLAM is distinguishing between the moving parts of a scene and the permanent background. Existing solutions generally fall into two categories, both of which have significant limitations:

  1. Semantic-Based Methods: These rely on pre-trained object detectors or segmentation models (like Mask R-CNN or SAM) to mask out categories known to move, such as humans or cars. However, these methods are often "blind" to objects outside their pre-defined categories. For example, a chair being moved would be ignored because a chair is typically categorized as a static object. Conversely, a person sitting perfectly still would be masked out unnecessarily, depriving the system of useful tracking data.
  2. Uncertainty and Rendering-Based Methods: Some 3DGS SLAM systems attempt to identify dynamic regions by looking for areas where the map reconstruction is poor (high rendering loss). The theory is that moving objects will look "wrong" in a static map. However, this approach is circular: if the map itself is poorly reconstructed due to low lighting or textureless surfaces, the system may incorrectly label static background as dynamic. Furthermore, if a dynamic object stops moving momentarily, these systems may mistakenly incorporate it into the permanent map.

GGD-SLAM moves beyond these limitations by focusing on the intrinsic nature of motion itself: a change in position over time. By incorporating temporal context—looking at a sequence of frames rather than just one—the system can identify dynamic behavior regardless of the object's semantic category.

Core Architecture: The Generalized Motion Model

The defining feature of GGD-SLAM is the Generalized Motion Model (GMM). Unlike per-scene models that need to "learn" a specific environment, the GMM is trained on motion datasets (like Davis) to recognize the features of moving objects generally.

Temporal Feature Extraction

To capture motion, GGD-SLAM maintains a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) queue QtQ_tQt​ that stores features from the previous LLL frames. When a new image ItI_tIt​ arrives, it is processed by a feature extractor (DINOv2) to produce current features xtx_txt​. The system then compares xtx_txt​ against the historical features in the queue QtQ_tQt​.

Sequential Attention Mechanism

The system uses a sequential attention mechanism to correlate the current frame with its history. The current features act as a "query" to find relevant motion cues in the historical "keys" and "values." This process produces a temporally enhanced feature Fattn,tF_{attn,t}Fattn,t​. To further refine this, GGD-SLAM uses a gated attention mechanism to separate dynamic and static signals. It calculates an enhancement coefficient DDD for dynamic attributes and a suppression coefficient SSS for static components. These are fused using the following operation:

Robust Tracking and Mapping

The motion mask MtM_tMt​ generated by the GMM is used to shield the rest of the SLAM system from dynamic interference.

Tracking with Dynamic Masking

For tracking, GGD-SLAM estimates the camera's pose TtT_tTt​ using a dense bundle adjustment (DBA) framework. In a standard system, every pixel contributes to the pose calculation. In GGD-SLAM, the system calculates a static weight St=1−MtS_t = 1 - M_tSt​=1−Mt​. During optimization, the residuals in dynamic regions are effectively multiplied by zero. This prevents the moving objects from "pulling" the camera trajectory away from its true path. To handle the lack of stereo depth in a monocular setup, the system integrates scale-aware depth estimates DestD_{est}Dest​ from a foundation model (Metric3D-v2).

Mapping and Uncertainty

In the mapping phase, the system updates the 3D Gaussians. To prevent dynamic objects from being baked into the map, GGD-SLAM employs an uncertainty-aware MLP. This MLP predicts an uncertainty map UtU_tUt​. While previous methods relied solely on rendering error to drive this uncertainty, GGD-SLAM uses the GMM's motion mask as a prior. This ensures that even if a moving object has low rendering error (for example, if it is a flat, featureless surface), it is still marked as highly uncertain and excluded from the permanent map.

Refinement: Occlusion Recovery and Adaptive SSIM

Removing dynamic objects from a scene leaves "holes" in the map where the background was occluded. GGD-SLAM introduces two specialized techniques to maintain map consistency.

Occlusion Recovery with KD-Trees

When a dynamic object moves and reveals a previously hidden background, the system needs to fill that area correctly. GGD-SLAM maintains a KD-tree of all existing "static" Gaussians. For areas revealed by moving objects, the system queries the nearest static neighbors in the 3D map. It then samples from these neighbors to initialize new Gaussians, effectively "inpainting" the background with logically consistent geometry and color.

Distractor-Adaptive SSIM Loss

The Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) is a standard loss function used to ensure rendered images look like the inputs. However, in dynamic scenes, a moving object in the input image will conflict with the static rendered map, creating a massive loss that can corrupt the static map's parameters.

As shown in Figure 3, GGD-SLAM introduces a modified version of this loss. Instead of a standard uniform kernel, it uses an "adapted kernel" wadw_{ad}wad​ derived from the static mask. This ensures the SSIM is only computed over pixels that are truly static, preventing dynamic "distractors" from polluting the optimization of the background.

Performance and Results

The researchers evaluated GGD-SLAM on several benchmark datasets, including TUM RGB-D, Bonn RGB-D, and Wild-SLAM. These datasets contain challenging scenarios like crowded rooms, moving boxes, and fast camera motions.

Tracking Accuracy

In quantitative tests, GGD-SLAM achieved state-of-the-art results for monocular systems. In many cases, its tracking accuracy (measured by Absolute Trajectory Error or ATE) was competitive with systems that use RGB-D cameras (which have a built-in depth sensor). For example, on the bonn/crowd2 sequence—a highly dynamic scene—GGD-SLAM significantly outperformed existing monocular 3DGS methods like Splatam and MonoGS, which often failed to track the camera correctly due to the overwhelming presence of moving people.

Mapping Quality

Qualitatively, the maps produced by GGD-SLAM are noticeably cleaner. While other methods often leave "smears" or "floaters" where a person walked through the frame, GGD-SLAM successfully reconstructs the static background as if the person were never there. On the TUM and Bonn datasets, GGD-SLAM consistently achieved the highest PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio), indicating superior image reconstruction quality in static regions.

Figure 4: Qualitative comparison of mapping quality. GGD-SLAM (far right) produces the cleanest static background reconstructions compared to other monocular and even some RGB-D based methods.

Conclusion and Future Directions

GGD-SLAM represents a significant step forward for monocular dense SLAM. By moving away from specific semantic labels and instead leveraging generalizable temporal motion cues, it achieves a level of robustness previously difficult to reach without specialized hardware. Its core contributions—the Generalized Motion Model, the distractor-adaptive SSIM, and the KD-tree occlusion recovery—allow it to maintain high-fidelity maps and accurate tracking even in the midst of chaotic, real-world motion.

While the system currently focuses on reconstructing the static background by filtering out dynamics, the authors note that future work could involve explicitly modeling the motion of the dynamic objects themselves. This would allow for a complete representation of the scene, capturing both the permanent architecture and the transient actors within it. For now, GGD-SLAM provides a reliable foundation for robots and AR devices to operate in the complex, ever-changing environments of the real world.
