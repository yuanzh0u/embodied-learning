When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs

Abstract

Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) promise to ground language instructions in robot control, yet in practice often fail to faithfully follow language. When presented with instructions that lack strong scene-specific supervision, VLAs suffer from counterfactual failures: they act based on vision shortcuts induced by dataset biases, repeatedly executing well-learned behaviors and selecting objects frequently seen during training regardless of language intent. To systematically study it, we introduce LIBERO-CF, the first counterfactual benchmark for VLAs that evaluates language following capability by assigning alternative instructions under visually plausible LIBERO layouts. Our evaluation reveals that counterfactual failures are prevalent yet underexplored across state-of-the-art VLAs. We propose Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG), a simple yet effective dual-branch inference scheme that explicitly regularizes language conditioning in VLAs. CAG combines a standard VLA policy with a language-unconditioned Vision-Action (VA) module, enabling counterfactual comparison during action selection. This design reduces reliance on visual shortcuts, improves robustness on under-observed tasks, and requires neither additional demonstrations nor modifications to existing architectures or pretrained models. Extensive experiments demonstrate its plug-and-play integration across diverse VLAs and consistent improvements. For example, on LIBERO-CF, CAG improves π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​ by 9.7% in language following accuracy and 3.6% in task success on under-observed tasks using a training-free strategy, with further gains of 15.5% and 8.5%, respectively, when paired with a VA model. In real-world evaluations, CAG reduces counterfactual failures of 9.4% and improves task success by 17.2% on average.

AI Overview

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a prominent paradigm for robotic control, enabling agents to interpret natural language instructions and execute corresponding physical actions. By leveraging pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), these systems can potentially generalize across diverse tasks and environments. However, a significant challenge in their deployment is the tendency to prioritize visual cues over linguistic intent—a phenomenon where the model defaults to well-learned behaviors associated with a scene rather than following specific instructions. This behavior is particularly evident in "counterfactual" scenarios, where a task is visually plausible but differs from the most common training data for a given environment.

Figure 1: An overview of counterfactual failures in VLAs. Models often ignore instructions like "Pick up the mustard" to execute a more common training task like "Pick up the tape" due to vision-dominated biases. The proposed LIBERO-CF benchmark evaluates these failures, and Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG) is introduced to mitigate them.

The Challenge of Vision-Dominated Bias

The core issue addressed in this research is the "counterfactual failure," where a VLA disregards a specific instruction in favor of a "vision shortcut." In many robotic datasets, scenes are often paired with a limited set of tasks. If a robot is frequently trained to pick up a specific object in a certain scene, the model develops a strong associative link between that visual observation and a particular action.

From a Bayesian perspective, an ideal robotic policy determines an action aaa given an observation ooo and an instruction lll according to the following factorization:

In this equation, P(a∣o)P(a|o)P(a∣o) represents the vision-only prior—the probability of an action based solely on the scene—while P(l∣a,o)P(l|a,o)P(l∣a,o) represents the likelihood that the language instruction matches the action being taken. The researchers observe that in practice, the action distribution often collapses such that p(a∣o,l)≈p(a∣o)p(a|o,l) \approx p(a|o)p(a∣o,l)≈p(a∣o). This indicates that the language component has a marginal influence on the final action prediction, especially for instructions Lout,oL_{out,o}Lout,o​ that were under-observed during training compared to the dominant tasks Lin,oL_{in,o}Lin,o​.

This bias is driven by two main factors. First, robotic datasets are often visually dominant; while visual and action data are abundant, the variety of textual descriptions for a fixed scene is typically low. Second, architectural choices often exacerbate this: many VLAs process hundreds of visual tokens but only dozens of language tokens, potentially marginalizing the influence of text in the attention layers of the underlying transformer.

Characterizing Counterfactual Failures

To quantify this phenomenon, the researchers conducted ablation studies and visualizations. In standard benchmarks, VLAs often maintain high performance even when language input is removed entirely, yet they fail almost completely when visual input is removed. Visualizations of grasping distributions (Figure 2a) show that even when given empty or counterfactual instructions, a VLA's grasp points remain clustered around the objects involved in its primary training tasks.

Figure 2: (a) Visualizations of grasp distributions showing that models target training-task objects even under counterfactual or empty instructions. (b) Removing training-task objects significantly improves the model's ability to follow counterfactual instructions, confirming that these objects act as visual distractors.

Further experiments revealed that the presence of specific "training-task objects" acts as a visual attractor. For instance, if a model was trained to "Pick up the black bowl," and it is later asked to "Pick up the cookie box" in the same scene, it often defaults to the bowl. Interestingly, if the bowl is physically removed from the scene, the model’s success rate on the counterfactual task ("Pick up the cookie box") increases significantly, demonstrating that the visual presence of the bowl was actively biasing the model away from following the instruction.

The LIBERO-CF Benchmark

To systematically evaluate these failures, the researchers introduced LIBERO-CF, a benchmark specifically designed for counterfactual robotic tasks. Built upon the existing LIBERO environment, LIBERO-CF assigns alternative, visually plausible instructions to existing scene layouts that were either unseen or under-observed during the model's initial training.

The benchmark consists of four distinct suites:

  1. CF-Spatial (15 tasks): Evaluates spatial language grounding by asking the robot to interact with objects that were previously considered background elements or required different spatial reasoning.
  2. CF-Object (10 tasks): Tests object-centric instructions involving different target objects within the same scene.
  3. CF-Long (10 tasks): Measures the model's ability to follow long-horizon, multi-step instructions that involve new targets.
  4. CF-OOD (15 tasks): Evaluates generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) objects that were never seen during the model's finetuning.

Evaluation in LIBERO-CF focuses on two metrics: the "grounding rate" (whether the gripper makes contact with the correct target) and the "success rate" (whether the full task is completed). Crucially, the benchmark also distinguishes between "Faithful" behavior (following the new instruction) and "Biased" behavior (defaulting to the original training task).

Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG)

To mitigate vision shortcuts, the researchers developed Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG). CAG is an inference-time method inspired by classifier-free guidance used in generative models like Stable Diffusion. The key idea is to explicitly strengthen the conditioning on language by shifting the predicted action away from the vision-only (unconditional) prior and toward the language-conditioned prediction.

By increasing ω\omegaω, the system effectively "boosts" the importance of the language input relative to the visual prior, helping the model overcome the vision-dominated bias that causes it to default to training tasks.

Implementation Strategies for CAG

Implementing CAG requires a way to generate the unconditional action πuncond(a∣o,∅)\pi_{uncond}(a|o,\emptyset)πuncond​(a∣o,∅). The researchers proposed and evaluated two main strategies:

  1. Training-Free Strategy (TF): This method uses the standard VLA model for both branches. For the unconditional branch, the language input is simply dropped or replaced with a null token at inference time. This is the simplest approach as it requires no additional training.
  2. Vision-Action (VA) Prior: A separate, small vision-only model is trained on the same robotic dataset but without any language instructions. This model provides a "cleaner" vision-only prior, as it has never been exposed to the language modality.

Experimental results across multiple VLA architectures (including OpenVLA and π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​) showed that while the training-free strategy provides improvements, using an explicit VA Prior typically yields the most stable and significant performance gains.

Figure 4: Impact of the guidance scale ω\omegaω on grounding and success rates. Increasing ω\omegaω improves language grounding, though excessively high values can eventually lead to a decline in task success due to over-guidance.

The guidance scale ω\omegaω is a critical hyperparameter. As shown in Figure 4, increasing ω\omegaω generally improves the grounding rate, indicating that the model is more likely to target the correct object. However, if ω\omegaω is too high, the success rate may drop because the "over-guided" actions may become less physically coherent or drift from the precise control required for task completion.

Experimental Findings and Real-World Validation

The effectiveness of CAG was validated through extensive simulation in LIBERO-CF and real-world experiments using a Franka Research 3 robot arm.

In simulations, CAG consistently improved faithful success rates while reducing biased execution. For the π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​ model, CAG (using a VA Prior) improved the average success rate on counterfactual tasks from 13.2% to 21.7% in the LIBERO-CF benchmark. Simultaneously, it reduced the rate of biased execution—where the robot defaults to the original training task—by nearly 25%.

The real-world experiments (Figure 12) highlighted CAG's ability to improve language grounding in four key categories:

  • Object Recognition: Correctly identifying a specific object (e.g., "mustard" vs. "tape") in a cluttered scene.
  • Spatial Reasoning: Following instructions that specify location (e.g., "Pick up the cup on the right").
  • Goal Targeting: Completing specific multi-object interactions (e.g., "Put the cup in the basket").
  • Out-of-Distribution Generalization: Successfully interacting with novel objects that were not present in the training set.

Figure 12: Qualitative comparison between a baseline VLA (π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​) and the same model with CAG. CAG enables the robot to correctly identify objects and follow spatial instructions where the baseline model fails.

Crucially, the researchers found that CAG improves performance on counterfactual tasks without degrading performance on the original in-domain tasks. This indicates that CAG acts as a correction mechanism that "unblocks" the model's existing linguistic capabilities that were previously suppressed by visual shortcuts.

Significance for Robotic Learning

This work identifies a fundamental hurdle in creating reliable, general-purpose robotic agents. While large-scale pretraining gives VLAs strong multimodal priors, the data-dominant nature of visual observation can lead to models that are "lazy" followers of language. By providing both a benchmark (LIBERO-CF) to measure these failures and a practical, inference-time solution (CAG) to fix them, the research offers a path toward more trustworthy robotic systems.

The introduced Counterfactual Action Guidance is particularly notable because it is architecture-agnostic. It does not require retraining the large, computationally expensive VLA models themselves. Instead, it adjusts the way actions are selected at the moment of execution. This "plug-and-play" capability makes it a viable tool for enhancing a wide variety of existing and future robotic models, ensuring they remain faithful to human instructions even when those instructions go against the visual "grain" of the environment.
