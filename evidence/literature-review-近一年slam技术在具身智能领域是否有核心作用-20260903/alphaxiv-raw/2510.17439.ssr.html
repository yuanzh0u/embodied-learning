Abstract

Existing vision-language-action (VLA) models act in 3D real-world but are typically built on 2D encoders, leaving a spatial reasoning gap that limits generalization and adaptability. Recent 3D integration techniques for VLAs either require specialized sensors and transfer poorly across modalities, or inject weak cues that lack geometry and degrade vision-language alignment. In this work, we introduce FALCON (From Spatial to Action), a novel paradigm that injects rich 3D spatial tokens into the action head. FALCON leverages spatial foundation models to deliver strong geometric priors from RGB alone, and includes an Embodied Spatial Model that can optionally fuse depth, or pose for higher fidelity when available, without retraining or architectural changes. To preserve language reasoning, spatial tokens are consumed by a Spatial-Enhanced Action Head rather than being concatenated into the vision-language backbone. These designs enable FALCON to address limitations in spatial representation, modality transferability, and alignment. In comprehensive evaluations across three simulation benchmarks and eleven real-world tasks, our proposed FALCON achieves state-of-the-art performance, consistently surpasses competitive baselines, and remains robust under clutter, spatial-prompt conditioning, and variations in object scale and height.

AI Overview

Introduction to FALCON: Bridging the 2D-3D Gap in Robotics

Modern robotics research aims to develop generalist robot policies—models that can take natural language instructions and visual inputs to perform a variety of physical tasks. Many current approaches rely on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are built upon large-scale Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like Kosmos-2 or Flamingo. These models excel at semantic reasoning, such as identifying a "red apple" and understanding its relation to a "kitchen counter." However, a significant limitation exists: these VLMs are primarily pre-trained on 2D internet-scale data.

The physical world is 3D, and robot manipulation tasks—such as picking up an object of a specific height or placing a spoon between two other items—require precise 3D spatial reasoning. While 2D-centric VLAs are effective at high-level semantic tasks, they often struggle with fine-grained geometric details, depth perception, and spatial relations. This "spatial reasoning gap" can lead to failures when a robot encounters objects of different scales or needs to navigate cluttered environments.

Figure 1: FALCON (From Spatial to Action) demonstrates improved performance across various benchmarks by integrating 3D spatial foundation priors, showing strong modality transferability and enhanced spatial understanding.

FALCON (From Spatial to Action) is a framework designed to ground VLA models in spatial foundation priors. It introduces a specialized architecture that extracts robust 3D information from visual inputs and integrates it into the robot's action-generation process without disrupting the model's existing language-understanding capabilities. By doing so, FALCON provides a method for robots to utilize 3D spatial cues—even when only 2D images are available—while remaining flexible enough to incorporate higher-fidelity 3D data like depth maps when they are provided.

The Challenge of 3D Integration in VLA Models

Previous research has attempted to inject 3D information into VLA models using two primary methods. The first involves direct 3D input, where models consume point clouds or depth maps. While accurate, these methods are often tied to specific sensor setups, making them difficult to generalize to different robots or environments. This lacks "modality transferability," as the model might fail if a depth sensor is unavailable.

The second method involves "weak 3D cues," such as adding learnable spatial embeddings or pseudo-depth estimates to the VLM's input. However, these cues often lack the detail needed for complex manipulation. Furthermore, injecting these spatial tokens directly into the VLM's main processing layers can cause "embedding drift," where the new spatial information interferes with the pre-trained vision-language alignment, potentially degrading the model's ability to follow complex language instructions.

FALCON addresses these challenges by separating the semantic understanding (handled by the VLM) from the spatial grounding (handled by a new component called the Embodied Spatial Model). These two streams of information are then fused in a specialized action head, ensuring that the model's high-level reasoning remains intact while its actions become spatially aware.

1. Vision-Language Model (VLM)

FALCON uses a pre-trained VLM (such as Kosmos-2) to process images and text. The model tokenizes the input and appends a special action token tactt_{act}tact​. The output of this token, t^act\hat{t}_{act}t^act​, represents the semantic action intent of the model—essentially what the model thinks it should do based on the instruction and the 2D scene.

2. Embodied Spatial Model (ESM)

While the VLM handles semantics, the ESM focuses on geometry. It processes the side-view image and optional 3D data to produce a set of spatial tokens TsplT_{spl}Tspl​. These tokens capture the global 3D geometric structure of the environment. The ESM is trained using spatial foundation models to ensure it has a deep understanding of depth and spatial relations.

3. Spatial-Enhanced Action Head

This is where the semantic intent from the VLM and the geometric information from the ESM meet. Instead of feeding spatial tokens back into the VLM, FALCON combines them here to predict the final robot actions. This prevents the spatial data from "confusing" the VLM's linguistic reasoning.

The Embodied Spatial Model (ESM)

The ESM is the core component that provides FALCON with its spatial awareness. It is built to be flexible across different sensing modalities.

Training and Optimization

FALCON's training follows a two-stage process. First, the pre-trained VLM and ESM are kept frozen, and only the lightweight adapter is trained to align the spatial features with the VLM's semantic space. In the second stage, the VLM and the adapter are unfrozen and fine-tuned together on robotic data, while the ESM remains frozen to preserve its general spatial priors.

The training objective is to minimize a combined loss function:

L=Lpose+λLstate\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{pose} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{state}L=Lpose​+λLstate​

Here, Lpose\mathcal{L}_{pose}Lpose​ is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) for the gripper's 6-DoF position and orientation, and Lstate\mathcal{L}_{state}Lstate​ is the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) for the open/close state. The parameter λ\lambdaλ weights the importance of the gripper state relative to the pose.

Experimental Results and Performance

FALCON was evaluated in both simulation (CALVIN and SimplerEnv) and real-world robot settings. It was compared against several state-of-the-art baselines, including RT-1, OpenVLA, and SpatialVLA.

Simulation Performance

On the CALVIN benchmark, which tests long-horizon manipulation, FALCON achieved state-of-the-art results. In the zero-shot setting (generalizing to new environments), FALCON outperformed previous models that even had access to ground-truth 3D point clouds. This suggests that the ESM's implicit 3D priors are highly effective for manipulation.

Real-World Tasks and Spatial Reasoning

In real-world experiments, FALCON showed a significant lead in success rates. For "Base Tasks" involving picking and placing objects in cluttered scenes, FALCON achieved a 70% success rate, compared to 44.4% for SpatialVLA.

Figure 3: Success rates for various real-world robot manipulation tasks. FALCON consistently outperforms baseline models, especially in complex scenarios.

The model's spatial reasoning was specifically tested using variations in object size and height, as well as abstract spatial prompts (e.g., "place the fruit that is closest to the robot on the cutting board"). FALCON demonstrated remarkable robustness:

  • Object Scale: It successfully stacked blocks even when they were larger or smaller than those seen during training.
  • Varying Heights: It adapted to placing objects on surfaces of different heights without missing or colliding.
  • Abstract Prompts: It correctly interpreted relative terms like "closest" or "between," whereas other models often failed to identify the correct target.

Figure 4: FALCON's few-shot generalization capabilities in real-world scenes with unseen objects, instructions, and backgrounds.

Modality Transferability

One of the most important findings was FALCON's flexibility with sensors. When tested with only RGB images, FALCON still outperformed other RGB-only models. When additional depth information was provided at test time, its success rate in height-sensitive tasks increased from 60% to 80%, demonstrating that it can effectively "up-sample" its performance when better sensors are available.

Conclusion

FALCON addresses the critical "spatial reasoning gap" in current Vision-Language-Action models by grounding them in 3D spatial foundation priors. By using a decoupled architecture—where a dedicated Embodied Spatial Model provides geometric information to an action head—FALCON achieves high precision without compromising the semantic power of pre-trained VLMs. Its ability to work with varied sensor inputs and generalize to novel, spatially complex scenarios makes it a robust framework for developing the next generation of generalist robot policies.
